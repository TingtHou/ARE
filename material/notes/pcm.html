PcM 1.4Standard of care — the concept the whole exam orbits

The definition to be able to write out

What a reasonably prudent architect would do in the same general locale, in the same time frame, given the same or similar facts and circumstances. Or, in AIA B101–2007 §2.2 language: "the professional skill and care ordinarily provided by architects practicing in the same or similar locality under the same or similar circumstances."

  • The law does not require perfection. Minor inconsistencies in a document set do not by themselves breach the standard. More than one valid design exists; choosing a different detail is not a violation.
  • Most states require expert witness testimony to establish whether the standard was met — a lay judge or jury lacks the knowledge. The trier of fact applies whichever expert standard it finds most credible.
  • "Same general locale" gets latitude — snow load in the South, hurricane load in the north-central states. But knowledge of basic design concepts and of certain products (asbestos, fire-retardant plywood) is held to be nationwide. Specialists are compared to other specialists nationally: a stadium, museum or major hospital designer is measured against peer specialists states away.
  • "Same time frame" means the standard at the time of the project, not at the time of the dispute. An expert asserting a standard based on techniques that were nascent then is not persuasive.
  • "Same or similar circumstances" includes the owner’s budget and constraints, the project type and the architect’s experience. A small residential firm is not held to a national healthcare practice’s standard.

How the standard gets raised — and why you resist

The two-reason answer

When an item asks why an architect should resist an owner’s heightened standard-of-care clause, the complete answer is (1) the elevated standard is not well defined in the industry, so it becomes a matter of debate, and (2) it is generally not insurable. An answer choice that gives only one of those is weaker than the one that gives both.

PcM 1.4Negligence — the four elements

  1. Duty — the architect owed a legal duty to the claimant.
  2. Breach — failure to perform that duty, or doing something that should not have been done.
  3. Cause — the breach was the proximate cause of the harm, without an intervening cause.
  4. Damage — actual harm or damage resulted.

All four must be present. No damage, no negligence claim. The two most common legal theories asserted against architects are negligence and breach of contract.

Breach of contract — its four elements

(1) Formation of an agreement; (2) performance by one party; (3) breach by the other; (4) damages. Formation requires a mutual understanding of definite and certain terms. Damages for breach put the plaintiff back where they would have been had the contract not been breached; damages for negligence compensate to remedy the negligence.

Betterment — error vs. omission

An error is something done wrong (stairs not to code, requiring replacement). An omission is something left out (carpet the project needed but the drawings did not show). The architect is generally liable for the full cost of an error, but only a fraction of the cost of an omission — because the owner would have had to pay for the omitted item anyway, and courts will not give the owner something for free. Damages for an omission are typically limited to the premium a contractor charges for the change order.

PcM 1.2Third parties, privity, and vicarious liability

  • Before 1956, the doctrine of privity barred third-party actions — a litigant had to prove a direct contractual relationship. Since then many states extend a duty to third parties the architect can reasonably foresee will depend on non-negligent services: construction workers, passers-by, occupants. The law on privity varies state to state.
  • Employees are agents of the firm; the firm is responsible for their acts on a project. A possible exception is an employee acting clearly outside their role.
  • The architect’s consultants are independent consultants and may act as the architect’s agent. Each is held to its own discipline’s standard, but the architect may be found liable for them because the architect contracted for the whole design scope. Indemnity does not save the architect if the consultant is insolvent or under-insured.
  • Owner-retained consultants — the architect is usually not responsible, unless it agrees to be, or acts in a way that assumes responsibility, such as signing a Certificate for Payment or Certificate of Substantial Completion covering that work.
  • Joint ventures — courts commonly hold JV parties jointly and severally responsible for the JV’s actions; the JV can be sued collectively or the parties individually.
  • Contractor-led design-build — the architect subcontracts to the contractor, and the layer of checks and balances that protects the owner is compromised or absent.

Exemptions — three families

  1. Buildings not intended for human habitation or occupancy — some agricultural structures. The most common exemption.
  2. Residential structures, defined by size, height, occupants or construction cost. Watch cost definitions: a final cost that exceeds a preliminary estimate can void the exemption.
  3. Categories of practice — federal employees (deference to the Supremacy Clause); reciprocal exemptions with allied professions.
The exemption paradox

You do not have to be an architect to design an exempt structure — but a non-architect who "holds out" as an architect while advertising for or designing an exempt structure violates the statute. Related title rules: interns may not use "intern architect" or "architectural intern"; a firm with only one registered architect cannot use "Architects" in the plural; a fictitious business title is a firm name that does not indicate ownership.

The four most common violations, in order

1. Attribution of credit (most frequent) · 2. Accurate representation of qualifications · 3. Attainment or provision of examples of work · 4. Basic honesty. Each has an identifiable injured party. Section 2 · Finances, Risk & Development of Practice 29–35% · largest section

PcM 2.1The annual financial plan — four interrelated components

  1. Revenue projection — anticipated revenue from projects under contract, in negotiation, and not yet obtained.
  2. Staffing plan — the size and cost of the staff needed to deliver that revenue.
  3. Overhead expense budget — the indirect cost of supporting that staff.
  4. Profit plan — the profit required to sustain the firm.

They are closely interrelated and must be developed concurrently. Two paths: start with the expected workload and derive the staff needed, or start with the staff you have and derive the workload it can support. A third begins with an achievable profit margin and works back.

Backlog — the early-warning instrument

Backlog is the sum of work still to be accomplished on existing projects. A shrinking backlog is the early warning sign of trouble: if it is not replenished at the rate revenue is drawn from it, a work shortfall follows. A growing backlog signals the need to hire — and hiring takes months. Outstanding proposals enter the projection with a probability factor: a $120,000 fee at 25% probability enters as $30,000.

PcM 2.1The three aspects of financial health

ProfitabilityAn excess of revenue over expenses. Required at three levels: for the firm (retained earnings for capital investment and downturns), for those who produce it (as reward), and for the owners (return on the risk they took).
LiquidityThe ability to convert an asset to cash with relative speed and ease and without significant loss in value.
SolvencyThe ability to meet financial obligations as they come due. Inability leads to insolvency and bankruptcy.
The cash cycle — draw this from memory

Cash → perform services → work in progress → bill → accounts receivable → collect → cash. Liquidity is maintained by invoicing regularly (turning WIP into receivables) and then collecting assiduously (turning receivables into cash). A profitable firm can still fail here — profit is not cash.

The overhead see-saw

As time charged to projects decreases, overhead increases — with a stable staff, every hour moved off a project becomes an indirect hour. Shifting staff from projects to overhead activities reduces revenue and raises overhead simultaneously. That is why utilisation and overhead rate move in opposite directions and why a low utilisation ratio forces a higher break-even multiplier.

The utilisation illusion

Utilisation is only a valid measure to the extent the firm actually gets paid for the direct labour. Charging time to a fixed-fee project whose fee is already spent makes utilisation look excellent — suggesting low overhead and high efficiency — while profit falls. High utilisation plus falling profit is the signature of a firm over-serving its fees.

Cash budgeting — five steps

  1. Forecast billings.
  2. Forecast the amount and timing of collections from those billings.
  3. Forecast cash receipts from other sources — investments, rents, asset sales.
  4. Forecast disbursements — payroll, consultants, other direct expenses, overhead, reimbursables and capital expenditures.
  5. Combine the schedules and compute the net monthly increase or decrease.

The ending cash balance of one month is the beginning balance of the next. If projections show a continuing deficit, the remedies are longer-term: refinance a major asset, add capital from existing or new partners, retain earnings rather than distributing all profit, or defer capital expenditure.

Short-term cash control levers, in the order a firm reaches for them: sustain backlog · bill promptly and correctly · pursue collections · control disbursements. The firm controls when it pays far better than when it is paid — it can defer vendors, reduce principals’ draws, and borrow short-term.

A slow-paying client is a warning, not just a nuisance

Clients slow to pay may be becoming dissatisfied or experiencing problems that do not bode well for the project. Treat a collection problem as a relationship signal before you treat it as an accounting one — and note that one of the leading causes of claims against architects is the architect’s own pursuit of fees, because owners counterclaim to force a compromise. The time and cost of defending frequently exceeds the fee recovered.

PcM 2.1Setting fees

Published fee curves were declared anticompetitive and illegal. They also rested on a fallacy — that two projects with the same construction budget and building type require the same effort.

  1. Calculate costs. Top-down budgeting starts from the assumed fee and allocates it across phases; bottom-up estimates time and cost task by task. The first bottom-up pass usually exceeds the expected fee; reconcile the two. Agreement between them is a good starting point — but not necessarily the right number to quote while still competing.
  2. Competitive analysis — research the going rate; check it with experienced non-competing firms.
  3. Risk analysis.
  4. Choose a fee method (see the Contracts page).
  5. Negotiate.

Claims avoidance in one sentence

A staggering proportion of construction claims rest wholly or partly on a failure to communicate, which leads to a failure to manage the other party’s expectations. The two habits that follow: remind everyone that the architect makes recommendations and the owner makes decisions, and that buildings, unlike automobiles, cannot be pre-tested.

The "100% complete" label often comes from owner–architect agreements tying fee to percentage complete — but a single set of construction drawings is unlikely to be 100 percent complete, and labelling it so creates an unachievable expectation of performance.

Explaining consequences, and "creative conflict"

It is not enough to disagree with a client decision. If the client deletes basement waterproofing, explain that the decision could result in water leaking into the basement, damaged finishes and expensive repair. As long as the consequences described represent your honest understanding, you are on solid ground. And raise it when the change is requested, not after it has been made.

Project documentation — the credibility rules

  • Write in ink.
  • Use bound or spiral notebooks — pages cannot be inserted later, which supports the claim that the notes are contemporaneous.
  • Number and date pages sequentially with no gaps.
  • Keep from day one: daily diary or site observation reports, photographs, correspondence, status letters (regular client updates), "issue" letters flagging significant changes or emerging problems, meeting minutes, memos of telephone conversations, schedules, change order logs, shop drawing logs, progress payment logs, estimates, bids, invoices.

PcM 2.2Insurance

Professional liability (errors and omissions)

  • Covers the firm’s liability for negligent acts, errors or omissions in the performance of professional services, within the policy’s geographic territory.
  • Defence costs are paid subject to the policy limit and deductible — that is, defence erodes the limit.
  • Where the policyholder selects and approves the defence attorney, the policyholder — not the insurer — is that attorney’s client.
  • Broad policies insure the firm plus any partner, officer, director, stockholder or employee acting within professional duties.
  • Two claim triggers: (1) receipt of a demand for money or services with an allegation of a wrongful act — broad enough to include an angry client phone call demanding you "fix it"; (2) the more subjective threat of an action or a troubling circumstance. Failure to report a claim in a timely manner may jeopardise coverage.
  • Consent to settle (the "hammer" clause): most policies require the insurer to have the insured’s consent before settling. If they disagree, the insured may be liable for any judgment plus defence costs above the amount at which the insurer could have settled; conversely the insurer may be liable above the amount the insured asked it to settle for.
  • Never offer to remedy a situation without the insurer’s approval — policies exclude coverage where the insurer was not involved in authorising a settlement.
What professional liability does NOT cover

Express warranties and guarantees. Certificates that operate as warranties without a known fact or qualified professional opinion. Indemnifying another party for that party’s own negligence. Contractual breaches are specifically excluded by underwriters. Dishonest acts of employees or associates (that needs a fidelity bond). A construction manager at risk acting as a general contractor — though a CM as adviser is covered under most professional liability policies. And it is not general liability: slips, falls and third-party property damage go to CGL.

Choosing an insurer

A.M. Best rates relative financial strength and the ability to meet contractual obligations — it is part of the annual AIA insurance survey and one of the AIA commendation requirements. Standard & Poor’s rates the ability to pay claims over time; Moody’s also rates financial strength. Admitted (licensed) carriers submit to state insurance regulation and their insureds are covered by state guarantee funds. Non-admitted (excess and surplus) carriers are unregulated as to coverage and rates — more flexibility and low premiums in soft markets, less availability in hard ones.

Premium factors: billing volume · types of services · project types (condominiums generate more claims) · the firm’s claims experience · geographic location · continuity and longevity credit · competition · the coverage-deductible-endorsement combination.

PcM 2.2Dispute avoidance and resolution

The three most powerful dispute-prevention tools, in order: a clear written agreement → clear communication throughout → early, direct negotiation when something goes wrong. The two contractual issues that cause the most avoidable disputes are an ill-defined scope of services and an ill-defined basis of compensation.

The full mediation / arbitration / litigation comparison table is on the Contracts page. Two additions worth knowing: a Special Master is a hybrid — a neutral expert appointed by a court to manage technical aspects of a construction dispute — and some agreements require compulsory direct negotiation between principals before any formal process begins.

PcM 2.3Strategy and the causes of firm failure

Marketing budget runs 5–7% of net fee revenue, higher when entering new markets, lower when serving existing clients. Repeat clients account for 60–85% of work at U.S. firms — which makes client retention the cheapest business development there is.

Ten causes of firm failure

  1. Inadequate capital — no cushion for a downturn or a slow-paying client
  2. Poor cash flow management — profitable on paper, out of money in fact
  3. Failure to collect receivables
  4. Uncontrolled overhead
  5. Over-expansion or growth faster than management capacity
  6. Over-reliance on a single client, market or project type
  7. Inadequate or absent financial management information
  8. Failure to price work to cover cost plus profit
  9. Loss of key personnel or failure to plan ownership transition
  10. A major uninsured or under-insured claim
The single-client trap

Concentration risk is the quietest of these. A firm with one client supplying 60% of revenue is not a stable business — it is a department of that client with worse job security. If an item describes a firm being offered a large project that would push one client over half its revenue, the risk-aware answer accounts for concentration, not just capacity.

What each method buys and costs

DBBBuys: price certainty through competitive bidding after design is essentially complete; clear, well-established risk allocation; the largest body of case law. Costs: the slowest method — fully sequential, no overlap; no contractor input during design on constructability, cost or schedule; adversarial potential because the contractor had no voice in the design.
CM as AdviserBuys: independent owner-side cost and schedule expertise from early design, without adding construction risk; the owner keeps direct contracts with trade contractors. Costs: no single point of construction responsibility and no price guarantee; more parties to coordinate; the CM has no skin in the game because it does not hold the GMP.
CM as ConstructorBuys: a single point of construction responsibility plus early contractor input; price certainty via GMP; fast-track capability. Costs: the GMP is typically set when CDs are only 60–70% complete, which generates disputes over what was included; and the CM’s advisory role during preconstruction conflicts with its later at-risk role — it is advising on its own future price.
Design-BuildBuys: single-point responsibility for design and construction; overlap of design and construction; one contract to administer. Costs: the owner loses direct control of and relationship with the architect; design quality can lose to cost-cutting when one at-risk entity controls both; the architect as subconsultant loses the independent-checker role that protects the owner.
IPDBuys: the best alignment of incentives; earliest collaboration; less waste and change-order friction. Costs: requires a sophisticated owner and high trust; unfamiliar liability and insurance structures; little legal precedent.
Two definitions people mix up

Bridging is not a delivery method on its own — it is a design-build variant. The owner first retains a criteria (bridging) architect to take the design to a defined level, usually design development, establishing performance criteria and design intent; the owner then contracts with a design-build entity (with its own architect of record) to finish the design and build to those criteria. It gives the owner more design control while keeping single-point construction risk transfer.

Fast-track is not a delivery method either — it is a scheduling strategy layered onto CMc, design-build or occasionally CMa. Construction starts on early bid packages (site work, foundations, steel) before the full CD set is complete, so later design runs parallel to earlier construction. It requires reliable early cost estimating and someone positioned to manage incremental bid packages, and it raises the risk of costly redesign if a later decision affects work already built.

The "at risk" label is misleading — and NCARB knows it

Your handbook flags the "CM at risk" vs. "CM not at risk" distinction as ambiguous. A CMc engaged without a GMP still performs the work and can still take a loss — it is "at risk" in that sense. It is the presence of a GMP that creates the specific price-guarantee risk, not the label. The cleaner distinction: does the construction manager hold the construction contract? CMa no, CMc yes.

GMP mechanics

  • Committed when construction documents are typically 60–70% complete, based on the CM’s evolving estimates.
  • A GMP contract must include a defined scope of work against which the guarantee applies — without it the guarantee is illusory.
  • Savings-split provisions are common: if the actual cost of the work comes in under the GMP, savings are split between owner and CMc per a negotiated formula.

PcM 3.3Client and project selection as a risk instrument

Go / no-go is the cheapest risk control available, because it costs nothing to decline. Weigh: does the firm have the expertise (Rule 3.102)? the capacity? Is the client experienced, solvent and reasonable? Is the fee commensurate with the risk? Does the contract allocate risk defensibly? Does this project fit the firm’s strategic direction? Would it create client concentration?

When the firm lacks the expertise

The three defensible responses are: decline the commission, associate with a firm that has the expertise, or retain a consultant who has it. "Accept and learn as you go" is always wrong — it breaches both AIA Rule 3.102 and the standard of care.

The details that generate items

  • Professional corporation (PC): formed specifically to provide professional services; normally must be owned or at least controlled by professionals licensed in the state; the corporate form usually does NOT protect individual stockholders from professional liability.
  • Foreign corporation: a corporation practising in a state other than its state of incorporation must register as a foreign corporation there — failing to do so can jeopardise legal rights including the right to sue to collect fees.
  • Partnership default rules (Uniform Partnership Act): all partners share income and losses equally; statutes do not usually recognise unequal contributions, and do not treat contributed services as equivalent to cash. On failure, state law allocates losses by capital contribution and ignores contributed services.
  • Incoming partners are generally liable only for debts incurred after they joined — but their capital is subject to claims by prior creditors. Departing partners remain liable for all debts incurred while they were partners.
  • Buy/sell agreement: the standard mechanism for ownership change in a corporation — requires departing shareholders to sell back, keeping shares "in the family," and is often mandated by registration statutes. Ownership transition in a corporation is generally easier than in a partnership.
  • Compensation is not necessarily proportional to ownership — it is set by agreement. An employee offered shares should ask whether the cost is justified by increased compensation, whether share value will increase, and whether control will increase; with no market for the shares and no buy/sell agreement, shares may have little investment value.
  • Contractor’s licence: architects engaged in general contracting — as developers, design-builders or construction managers — may need one in states where they will engage in construction.

PcM 4.2Processes, policies and resources for design and documentation

Objective 4.2 asks how a firm standardises its work so quality does not depend on who is in the room. The instruments:

  • Office standards — drawing conventions, sheet organisation, graphic standards, detail libraries, CAD/BIM protocols and naming, model-sharing agreements (AIA E203/E201).
  • Specification systems — MasterFormat organises by work result / trade section, used for the project manual and for detailed estimating. UniFormat organises by building system or assembly (substructure, superstructure, exterior enclosure) and suits early elemental estimating and design-phase cost control.
  • QA vs. QC — quality assurance is the system (the standards, procedures and training that prevent defects); quality control is the checking (the reviews that catch them). QA is preventive and firm-wide; QC is detective and project-specific.
  • Document retention — keep project records at least through the applicable statute of repose, and consistently: a retention policy applied unevenly is worse than none, because selective destruction invites an adverse inference.
  • Knowledge management — lessons-learned reviews, detail libraries, post-occupancy evaluation feeding back into standards.

The distinction

Four of the five options are pass-through entities. Only the C corp is a taxpayer in its own right, which is what produces double taxation — corporate tax on profit, then personal tax on the distribution. If a stem says “separately from,” “the company itself pays,” or “double taxation,” the answer is the C corp.

Distractor design

The option list is stacked four-to-one with pass-throughs, and three of them are near-synonyms (LLC / LLP / PLLP). That is deliberate: it makes the list feel like it is testing the differences among the pass-throughs, when it is testing the one entity that is not one.

Worth knowing

The quiz files this under Business Operations, but NCARB tests business structures under 4.1 Identify and compare practice and business structures, in Practice Methodologies. The quiz's content-area labels do not always match NCARB's objectives — go by the objective.

What the pyramid problem is

In a small firm the principals sit at the apex and every decision has to come back up to them. That bottleneck delays production and consumes the owner's time, which the firm also needs for business development, contracts and staffing. The owner is doing two jobs at once.

The named fix is the matrix solution: the principal appoints a project manager other than themselves, so decisions get made at the project level and the owner is freed for firm-level work.

Distractor design — the pattern to carry forward
