Section 4 · Project Integration of Program & Systems 32–38%

This section asks one question in many costumes: given all the constraints at once, which arrangement works? The reliable way to answer is a fixed order of operations, because the constraints are not equally binding.

The order of operations for any integration item1. Code envelope (occupancy, construction type, allowable height and area) → 2. Zoning envelope (take the lower of the two) → 3. Structural grid (span the programme needs) → 4. Floor-to-floor (structure + MEP + ceiling + clearance) → 5. Core and egress (exits, distance, separation) → 6. Programme fit → 7. Cost. Anything that fails at step 1 or 2 is illegal, and no amount of elegance later rescues it. Items are built to tempt you into solving step 6 first.

PPD 4.2Floor-to-floor: the calculation the section is built on

If you learn one integration skill, learn this one. It connects structure, MEP, code and zoning in a single number, and it is the mechanism behind most "why doesn't this fit" items.

Worked example — building up a floor-to-floor dimension
A 90,000 SF speculative office, 30 ft × 30 ft bays, VAV all-air system. Ceiling height required by the client 9 ft 0 in Ceiling plenum: Structural depth — composite steel beam at span/22 30 ft × 12 = 360 in; 360 ÷ 22 = 16.4 in → use W18 18 in Metal deck + concrete topping 5 in Duct depth at the largest run (main trunk) 18 in Duct-to-structure clearance / hangers 4 in Sprinkler main + branch below duct 6 in Light fixture depth + ceiling grid 4 in Plenum subtotal 55 in = 4 ft 7 in FLOOR-TO-FLOOR = 9 ft 0 in + 4 ft 7 in = 13 ft 7 in round to 13 ft 8 in Now test it against zoning: Height limit 120 ft. 120 ÷ 13.67 = 8.78 → 8 storeys. 8 × 11,250 SF/floor = 90,000 SF ✓ fits exactly. Now the tested move — the engineer needs a 24 in trunk, not 18 in: Plenum becomes 61 in; floor-to-floor 14 ft 1 in 120 ÷ 14.08 = 8.52 → still 8 storeys. Survives. And again — the client wants 10 ft ceilings: Floor-to-floor 15 ft 1 in; 120 ÷ 15.08 = 7.96 → 7 storeys. 7 × 11,250 = 78,750 SF. You have just lost 11,250 SF of leasable area to one foot of ceiling height.
The three moves that buy back floor-to-floor1. Run ducts through the structure instead of under it — castellated or cellular beams, open-web joists, or beam penetrations. 2. Use a flatter structure — post-tensioned flat plate at roughly span/45 instead of a beam-and-slab system. 3. Move the ducts out of the ceiling — underfloor air, or a DOAS with local terminal units. Each is a genuine exam answer to "how do you get another storey inside the height limit."

Correction — one-way vs two-way slab, from your JX notes

They state "One-way slab (< 2.0) … Two-way slab (≥ 2.0)." The inequalities are swapped. A slab acts one-way when the long span ÷ short span is 2 or more (load takes the short route almost entirely) and two-way when that ratio is less than 2. Memorise it as: a long skinny panel is one-way; a square-ish panel is two-way.

Honesty note: this is universal reinforced-concrete doctrine and appears in every textbook, but I was not able to pull a direct citation for the 2.0 ratio in this session. The direction of the inequality above is certainly right; if you want the citation, it is in ACI 318 commentary.

Section properties: I = bh³/12 for a rectangle; S = I/c, which for a rectangle is bh²/6; bending stress fb = M/S. Depth is cubed in I and squared in S — that is why depth beats width every time, and why the answer to "the beam deflects too much" is almost always "make it deeper," not "make it wider."

Worked example — sizing a steel beam from scratch
Simply supported steel beam. Span L = 30 ft. Tributary width 10 ft. Floor live load 50 psf (office), superimposed dead load 30 psf. Step 1 — line load on the beam Total load = 50 + 30 = 80 psf w = 80 psf × 10 ft tributary = 800 lb/ft = 0.8 k/ft Step 2 — maximum moment M = wL²/8 = 0.8 × 30² / 8 = 0.8 × 900 / 8 = 90 kip-ft Convert: 90 × 12 = 1,080 kip-in Step 3 — required section modulus Allowable bending stress, compact and braced ≈ 24 ksi S required = M / Fb = 1,080 / 24 = 45 in³ Step 4 — pick a section Any W-shape with Sx ≥ 45 in³. A W16×31 has Sx = 47.2 in³. ✓ Step 5 — sanity-check against the rule of thumb span/22 → 30 × 12 / 22 = 16.4 in. A W16 is 16 in nominal. The rule of thumb and the calculation agree — which is the point of the rule of thumb. Step 6 — deflection check Floor member live-load limit is L/360 (IBC Table 1604.3) Allowable = 30 × 12 / 360 = 1.0 in Live-load w = 50 × 10 = 500 lb/ft = 0.0417 k/in; L = 360 in Δ = 5wL⁴/384EI. With Ix = 375 in⁴ for W16×31, E = 29,000 ksi: Δ = 5(0.0417)(360⁴) / (384 × 29,000 × 375) = 1.30 in 1.30 in > 1.0 in allowable → FAILS deflection. Go one size up: W18×35, Ix = 510 in⁴ Δ = 5(0.0417)(360⁴) / (384 × 29,000 × 510) = 0.96 in ✓ RESULT: strength said W16×31; deflection governed and gave W18×35. For floor beams, deflection governs far more often than stress — that is the lesson, and it is examinable.

Diaphragms. A diaphragm is flexible when its maximum deflection exceeds twice the average story drift of the shear walls it connects, and rigid when it does not. Verified The consequence is how load is distributed: a flexible diaphragm distributes by tributary area; a rigid diaphragm distributes by relative stiffness and also picks up torsion. Wood and untopped steel deck tend to be flexible; concrete tends to be rigid.

Seismic base shear is V = CsW — a coefficient times the effective seismic weight. The R factor rewards ductility: a higher R (special moment frame) reduces design base shear relative to a low-R brittle system. Heavier building means more seismic force, which is why concrete attracts more seismic load than steel of the same size.

Plan and vertical irregularities you can see Verified, ASCE 7-16Horizontal: torsional irregularity (drift at one end > 1.2× the average; extreme > 1.4×), re-entrant corner (both projections beyond the corner exceed 15% of the plan dimension), diaphragm discontinuity (opening > 50% of the diaphragm area), out-of-plane offset, non-parallel systems. R-values add. U-values do not.

Add the R of every layer including both air films, then invert once at the end. Never average U-values across different assemblies by simple arithmetic — weight them by area. This is the single most common envelope-calculation error.

Worked example — U-factor, heat loss, and where the dew point lands
Wall assembly, outside to inside: Outside air film R 0.17 Brick veneer, 4 in R 0.44 Air space, 1 in R 1.00 Rigid insulation, 2 in @ R-5/in R 10.00 CMU, 8 in R 1.11 Gypsum board, 5/8 in R 0.56 Inside air film R 0.68 R total = R 13.96 U = 1 / 13.96 = 0.0716 Btu/h·ft²·°F HEAT LOSS through 4,000 SF of this wall Indoor 70°F, outdoor design 10°F → ΔT = 60°F Q = U × A × ΔT = 0.0716 × 4,000 × 60 = 17,184 Btu/h WHERE DOES THE DEW POINT LAND? Temperature drop across each layer is proportional to its share of R total. Total drop = 60°F, so each R unit = 60/13.96 = 4.30 °F per unit of R. Interface R accumulated Temp Inside surface of gypsum 0.68 70 − 2.9 = 67.1°F Gypsum / CMU 1.24 70 − 5.3 = 64.7°F CMU / insulation 2.35 70 − 10.1 = 59.9°F Insulation / air space 12.35 70 − 53.1 = 16.9°F Air space / brick 13.35 70 − 57.4 = 12.6°F Indoor air at 70°F and 35% RH has a dew point of about 41°F. The first interface colder than 41°F is the OUTBOARD face of the insulation (16.9°F). Everything inboard of the insulation stays well above dew point. CONCLUSION: with continuous exterior insulation, the vapour control layer belongs on the WARM (inboard) side of the insulation, and the assembly is safe. Move that insulation into the cavity behind the CMU and the CMU itself drops below dew point — which is why exterior continuous insulation is the durable detail.
Vapour control by climate — and MEEB's cautionHeating-dominated (cold) climates: vapour retarder on the interior, warm side. Hot-humid climates: the vapour drive reverses, so the retarder goes toward the exterior — and an interior polyethylene sheet becomes a moisture trap. MEEB frames this as placement relative to the dominant vapour drive, not a fixed rule. Never put two vapour-impermeable layers in one assembly — water that gets between them cannot leave in either direction. Your Panic Notes get the climate pairing right: hot-humid → outside; cold → inside.

Worked example — ventilation load and duct sizing consequence
A 3,000 SF open office. ASHRAE 62.1 ventilation for an office space is Rp = 5 cfm/person plus Ra = 0.06 cfm/ft², at a default occupant density of 5 people per 1,000 ft². Step 1 — occupancy 3,000 ÷ 1,000 × 5 = 15 people Step 2 — breathing-zone outdoor air Vbz = (Rp × people) + (Ra × area) = (5 × 15) + (0.06 × 3,000) = 75 + 180 = 255 cfm outdoor air Step 3 — sensible cooling airflow for the same space Space sensible load, say 60,000 Btu/h. Supply air at 55°F into a 75°F room → ΔT = 20°F Q = 1.1 × cfm × ΔT → cfm = Q ÷ (1.1 × ΔT) = 60,000 ÷ (1.1 × 20) = 2,727 cfm Step 4 — the point Outdoor air is 255 of 2,727 cfm — about 9%. The duct is sized by the COOLING airflow, not ventilation. Step 5 — why cooling, not heating, sets duct size Heating: supply 130°F into 70°F room, ΔT = 60°F cfm = 60,000 ÷ (1.1 × 60) = 909 cfm Cooling ΔT (20°F) is a third of heating ΔT (60°F), so cooling needs three times the air. DUCTS ARE ALWAYS SIZED FOR COOLING.

Systems MEEB 11th edition does not contain at all Supplied from outside
  • DOAS (dedicated outdoor air system). One system delivers 100% outdoor air, usually with energy recovery, and a separate system handles sensible load. It decouples latent from sensible control, so each can be optimised. It is the standard partner for radiant slabs, chilled beams, VRF and water-source heat pumps — none of which handle ventilation well alone. Verified conceptually I could not confirm a specific plenum-depth saving figure; do not memorise one.
  • Chilled beams. Passive beams rely on natural convection only. Active beams entrain room air with an integral primary-air nozzle, so they move more heat in the same size. Both need a DOAS for ventilation and both demand tight dew-point control, because a chilled beam that sweats drips on the occupants.
  • VRF — variable refrigerant flow. A distributed heat-pump system with individual zone control and simultaneous heating and cooling in different zones.
  • Radiant slab — hydronic tubing in the floor. Handles sensible load with high comfort and no ducts; slow to respond; needs separate ventilation and dehumidification.
  • Displacement ventilation — supplies cool air low and slow; buoyancy carries heat and contaminants up and out above the occupied zone. Needs higher ceilings and cannot handle a high sensible load alone.
  • ERV vs HRV — an HRV transfers sensible heat only; an ERV transfers sensible and latent (heat and moisture). ERV in humid climates, HRV where you do not want to move moisture.

MEEB's own: the heat pipe is a passive, no-moving-parts sensible exchanger between adjacent supply and exhaust streams. A runaround coil loop carries glycol between two coils when the streams are not adjacent. An economiser uses outdoor air (or water) directly for cooling when conditions allow. Parallel-blade dampers for on/off; opposed-blade dampers for throttling. Condenser water flow is roughly 3 gpm per ton.

Point method: illuminance at a point is E = I / d², with a cosine correction for angle. Horizontal illuminance on a desk not directly under the fixture is I × cos θ / d²; vertical is I × sin θ / d². Your Panic Notes state both correctly.

Daylight factor = interior illuminance ÷ unobstructed exterior horizontal illuminance × 100%. It resolves into Sky Component + Externally Reflected Component + Internally Reflected Component. MEEB's caution is examinable: daylight factor is valid only for overcast or defined CIE skies, not partly cloudy or direct sun, because the ratio is not constant then.

CRI and colour temperature — the counterintuitive pairCRI is how faithfully colours render, on a 0–100 scale against a reference at the same colour temperature. CCT is how the lamp looks, in kelvin — and it runs backwards from intuition: low K reads warm (2,700 K, orange), high K reads cool (5,000 K, blue). The reason is the blackbody curve — heat metal and it glows red, then yellow, then blue-white. Incandescent CRI = 100 by definition. High-pressure sodium is efficient and renders colour terribly (CRI ≈ 21 standard).

Worked example — reverberation time and how to fix it
Lecture room 40 ft × 30 ft × 12 ft = 14,400 cubic ft. Surfaces and absorption: Ceiling 40×30 = 1,200 SF, gypsum α 0.05 → 60 sabins Floor 40×30 = 1,200 SF, vinyl α 0.03 → 36 sabins Walls (40+30)×2×12 = 1,680 SF, gyp α 0.05 → 84 sabins Occupants, 60 people ≈4.0 ea → 240 sabins Total A = 420 sabins RT = 0.05 × 14,400 / 420 = 1.71 seconds Recommended for speech: roughly 0.6–1.0 s. 1.71 s is far too live — speech will smear. FIX — replace the gypsum ceiling with acoustic tile, α 0.70: New ceiling absorption = 1,200 × 0.70 = 840 sabins New total A = 840 + 36 + 84 + 240 = 1,200 sabins RT = 0.05 × 14,400 / 1,200 = 0.60 s ✓ Note what did the work: the ceiling is the largest single surface and had the most room to improve. Treating the floor (36 → maybe 120 sabins with carpet) would have moved RT only to 1.44 s. TREAT THE BIGGEST SURFACE FIRST.
The composite STC trap — Amber Book's best acoustics example

A wall rated STC 45 with windows at STC 26 covering 30% of its area has a composite STC of 31 — six points above the window, fourteen below the wall. An assembly performs at its weakest path, not its average. The analogy that makes it stick: keeping sound out is like keeping water out — the leak decides, not the roof. Any item that upgrades the wall while leaving the door undercut is a wrong answer.

Perception: 3 dB is barely perceptible, 6 dB is clearly noticeable, 10 dB reads as twice as loud. Confirmed in both MEEB and Jenny's notes Massive, airtight, structurally discontinuous assemblies stop airborne sound; structurally discontinuous and resilient floor assemblies stop impact noise. Low-frequency sound passes through walls more easily, bends around corners, builds in room corners, and needs thicker absorbers — or absorbers mounted over an air space.

Conveying, fire suppression, and the rest of the "special systems" list

Objective 3.3 names six: acoustics, communications, lighting, security, conveying, fire suppression. Expect check-all-that-apply items built on that list.

  • Elevators. Hydraulic — a piston, usually in a drilled hole; cheap, slow, limited to roughly 5–6 storeys, no penthouse machine room but a pit and a machine room at the base. Traction — ropes over a driven sheave with counterweights; fast, efficient, any height. MRL (machine-room-less) traction puts the machine in the hoistway. Sizing is by handling capacity in a five-minute peak and by interval; a rule of thumb is one elevator per 45,000–50,000 SF of office, but treat that as indicative.
  • Escalators — typically 30° incline, 32 or 48 in nominal width, and they occupy a great deal of floor area for the vertical rise they achieve. They are not a means of egress.
  • Fire suppression — wet pipe (default, fastest), dry pipe (unheated spaces), pre-action (double-interlock for data centres and archives, to prevent accidental discharge), deluge (open heads, high-hazard), and clean agent or water mist where water damage is unacceptable.
  • Standpipes — Class I 2½ in connections for the fire department, Class II 1½ in for occupant use, Class III both. Verified
  • Security — access control, intrusion detection, video surveillance, and the distinction between a credential reader (proximity card, MIFARE) and a biometric.
  • Communications — structured cabling, telecom rooms and their stacking, distribution frames, wireless coverage, and the space these actually need on each floor.

Corridor width = 147 × 0.15 = 22.1 in BUT the minimum corridor width is 44 in. → 44 in corridor. Step 4 — the point At this occupant load the CODE MINIMUMS govern, not the capacity calculation. Capacity only takes over when occupant load × factor exceeds the minimum: stairs: 44 ÷ 0.2 = 220 occupants corridor: 44 ÷ 0.15 = 293 occupants Below those numbers, answer 44 in. Step 5 — now make it an assembly floor instead 22,000 SF of unconcentrated assembly at 15 SF net: 22,000 ÷ 15 = 1,467 occupants Exits required: more than 1,000 → FOUR exits. Stair width = 1,467 × 0.2 = 293.4 in total, distributed across those stairs, and no one exit may carry so much that losing it drops available capacity below 50%.
The egress-capacity factors, and the condition that halves themStairways 0.3 in/occupant; all other egress components 0.2 in/occupant. Reduced to 0.2 and 0.15 where the building is sprinklered and has an emergency voice/alarm communication system, and is not Group H or I-2. Verified Both conditions are required. Sprinklers alone do not buy the reduction — that is the trap, and your Panic Notes omit the reduced factors entirely.

Correction — your Panic Notes say 5 feet

They state: "If your exterior wall has a fire separation distance from the neighboring building greater than 5', your fire rating only needs to account for interior fire exposure." The threshold is 10 feet, not 5. This is a straightforward factual error in a number you would otherwise memorise.

Correction — "1-hour walls get 1-hour doors" is not generally true

Your Panic Notes say: "4-hour walls (3-hour doors) separate connected buildings, 2-hour walls (1.5 hour doors) separate stairwells and elevator shafts, 1-hour walls (1-hour doors) divide different occupancies." The first two pairings are right. The third is wrong in the common case. A 1-hour shaft or exit enclosure does take a 1-hour door — but a 1-hour occupancy-separation fire barrier takes a ¾-hour (45-minute) door, and a 1-hour corridor wall takes a ⅓-hour (20-minute) door. Since "divide different occupancies" describes a fire barrier, the note's own example is the case where it is wrong.

I am stating the ¾-hour and ⅓-hour figures as the well-established pattern but flagging that I could not confirm them against 2021 code text this session — which is exactly why you should pull Table 716.1(2) yourself.

Allowable area — the formula

§506.2.1, single-occupancy: Aa = At + (NS × If) for buildings of three storeys or fewer, per storey; Aa = [At + (NS × If)] × Sa for more storeys, where Sa = 3 (or 4 if sprinklered under NFPA 13R). Verified against two independent sources including ICC's own training material

  • Aa = allowable area
  • At = tabular area factor from Table 506.2, for the building's actual sprinkler condition
  • NS = the nonsprinklered tabular factor — used in the frontage term regardless of the building's actual sprinkler status
  • If = frontage increase factor
  • Sa = storey multiplier
The NS trap

The frontage term uses NS, the nonsprinklered value, even in a fully sprinklered building. People substitute the sprinklered value there and get a much larger — and wrong — answer. Read the variable, not the building.

A genuine 2018 → 2021 change in the frontage increase

The 2015 and 2018 IBC used a formula: If = (F/P − 0.25)(W/30), where F is the perimeter fronting a public way or open space at least 20 ft wide, P is total perimeter, and W is the weighted average open-space width capped at 30 ft. If F/P is less than 0.25, If = 0. The 2021 IBC replaced this with a table lookup, Table 506.3.3. Change confirmed via ICC training material; I could not retrieve the 2021 table's values. The qualifying conditions survive: minimum 20 ft open space width, and at least 25% of the perimeter must front it before you get any increase at all.

Sprinkler height and storey increase, §504.2: tabular height and storey limits increase when sprinklered — but Group I-2 in Types IIB/III/IV/V and Groups H-1, H-2, H-3 and H-5 get no increase, and an NFPA 13R building is capped at 4 storeys and 60 feet regardless. Partially verified — I could not confirm the specific increase values.

PPD 2.3Integrating multiple codes

The governing principle is simple and heavily tested: where two applicable requirements differ, the more restrictive governs. Zoning against building code, IBC against accessibility standard, model code against local amendment — always the stricter.

The bodies you are integrating: IBC (life safety, construction), IEBC (existing buildings), IECC / ASHRAE 90.1 (energy), ICC A117.1 and the 2010 ADA Standards (accessibility — note the ADA is civil rights law, not a building code, and is enforced separately), NFPA 13/72/101, IPC / IMC, and the local zoning ordinance.

Where ICC A117.1 and the 2010 ADA Standards diverge

A117.1-2009 matched the ADA on turning space at 60 in. A117.1-2017 moved several dimensions: turning space to 67 in, clear floor space to 30 × 52 in, push-side door clearance to 52 in. Partially verified — from secondary commentary, not the ICC text itself. The practical answer on an integration item is always the same: satisfy the more stringent of the two. Compliance with one does not excuse the other, because they are enforced by different authorities.

Hard cost — site work, foundations, structure, envelope, MEP, interior finishes, general conditions, contractor overhead and profit, construction contingency. Soft cost — A/E and consultant fees, permits and plan review, legal, financing and interest during construction, insurance, taxes during construction, land, marketing and leasing, developer fee, FF&E, commissioning, owner's and design contingency.

Contingency AIA-published guidance, not a code figure: design contingency commonly 5–10% of construction cost, drawn down as design certainty rises; construction contingency commonly 5–10% under design-build; owner's contingency is risk-assessed individually — AIA's worked example allocated 3% for scope changes + 2% for risk mitigation + 1% project-specific = 6%.
