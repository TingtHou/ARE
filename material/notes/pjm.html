Project Management

How one project gets planned, contracted, executed and checked. Contracts carry the most weight — that material lives on the Contracts page; this page covers the other four sections and the project-side reading of the agreements.

Section 1 · Resource Management 7–13% · smallest section

PjM 1.1Assembling and allocating the team

  • Match staff level to task. The cost consequence is direct: a principal at a $169 billing rate doing production work a $85 technical staffer could do burns fee at twice the rate and lowers the net multiplier on the project.
  • Two organisational models in small firms: the pyramid — a principal at the top with staff assigned beneath, simple but dependent on one person; and the matrix — staff report both to a project manager and to a discipline or studio leader, which uses specialists better but creates competing demands on the same people.
  • Consultant selection criteria: technical qualification in the relevant discipline, experience with this building type, capacity and availability, adequate professional liability insurance, geographic reach, compatibility with the team, and fee. Qualification comes first — selecting a consultant on fee alone is the classic wrong answer.
  • Availability is a real constraint. If key staff are committed elsewhere, the honest responses are to phase the work, hire, subcontract, or decline — not to promise a schedule the firm cannot staff.
Where Resource Management meets PcM

Utilisation, billing rates and the staffing plan are PcM Section 2 material at firm scale; here they are project scale. The same arithmetic, a different unit of analysis. If a question gives you hours and rates for one project, you are in PjM — use the six-step project profit calculation on the Numbers page.

Section 2 · Project Work Planning 17–23%

PjM 2.1The work plan — nine elements

  1. Project scope and objectives — what is being delivered, and what is explicitly excluded.
  2. Deliverables by phase — what gets produced and issued, and when.
  3. Team organisation — who does what, internally and among consultants; roles and authority.
  4. Project schedule — milestones, durations, dependencies, review periods.
  5. Fee and budget allocation — the fee distributed across phases and tasks, with hours by staff level.
  6. Communication protocols — who talks to whom, meeting cadence, document routing, approval paths.
  7. Quality management plan — review points, who reviews, what gets checked.
  8. Risk assessment — the identified project risks and the response to each.
  9. Documentation and filing standards — the record-keeping structure for the project.
The work plan is a living document

It is written at the start and maintained — the objective says "develop and maintain." When scope, schedule or budget changes, the work plan is updated and the team re-briefed. An out-of-date work plan is how scope creep becomes invisible.

The two sides of project management

Managing the project — scope, schedule, budget, documents, the technical work — and managing the relationships — client expectations, team morale, consultant coordination, contractor dealings. Most failures are on the second side. The three clusters of activity are planning, facilitating and documenting.

PjM 2.2Scheduling

Bar / Gantt chartHorizontal bars against a calendar. Easy to read; does not show dependencies well.
Critical Path Method (CPM)A network of activities, durations and logical dependencies. The critical path is the longest sequence of dependent activities, and it determines the minimum possible project duration.
Float (slack)The time an activity can slip without delaying project completion. Free float is the slip that does not delay the succeeding activity. Activities on the critical path have zero float.
MilestoneA zero-duration marker event — permit issued, GMP set, substantial completion — used for tracking and often tied to payment.
The float question

A delay to a critical-path activity delays the project and may support a time-extension claim. A delay to an activity with float generally does not. That single sentence answers most CPM items. A corollary: shortening a non-critical activity does nothing for the completion date; to compress the project you must compress the critical path — and when you do, a different path may become critical.

The schedule must include allowances for the owner’s review periods, the owner’s consultants’ performance, and approvals by authorities having jurisdiction — B101 §3.1.3 says so explicitly. Once the owner approves it, neither party may exceed its time limits except for reasonable cause.

The rules of minutes

Minutes record decisions, action items with owners and dates, and unresolved issues — not a transcript. They are distributed promptly and are presumed accurate unless objected to within a stated period. That presumption is why the exam treats minutes as a risk-management instrument rather than a clerical chore: an unchallenged inaccurate minute becomes the record.

A related technique from your handbook: "creative conflict" — engaging in open debate makes participants more likely to recall the issue later. Faced with an aggressive schedule request, lay out the two alternatives honestly: accept the faster schedule with an acknowledged relationship between speed and the cost of mistakes, or maintain the present schedule. Raise it when the change is requested, not after it is done.

PjM 2.3Documentation and filing

A standard project filing structure, kept the same across projects, is itself a quality instrument — anyone can find anything, and nothing is lost when staff change. Typical top-level divisions: agreements and contracts · correspondence · meeting minutes · program and owner-furnished information · drawings and specifications by phase · consultant coordination · cost estimates · permits and approvals · bidding and procurement · construction administration (submittals, RFIs, changes, payment) · closeout.

Logs are the exam-relevant part: submittal log, RFI log, change order log, payment application log. Each shows what was received, when, what action was taken and when it was returned. A log is what proves the architect responded with reasonable promptness.

Section 3 · Contracts 25–31% · see the Contracts page

The full article-by-article treatment of B101, A101 and C401 is on the Contracts page, because it serves both divisions. What follows is the project-manager’s reading of the same material — the things a PM does with the agreement rather than the things a principal negotiates into it.

PjM 3.4Verifying adherence in practice

  • Know what you sold. Before the first meeting, the PM reads the executed agreement and extracts: the scope of basic services, everything listed as supplemental and to whom it is assigned, the deliverables by phase, the CA service limits in §4.2.3, the fee and its phase allocation, and the schedule commitments.
  • Watch the §4.2.3 counters. Number of submittal reviews, site visits, substantial-completion inspections and final inspections. When a limit is reached, the architect notifies the owner and further service becomes additional.
  • Recognise an additional service before performing it. The trigger list is in Contracts §4.2.1. The obligation is to notify the owner with reasonable promptness, explain the facts and circumstances, and obtain written authorisation before proceeding.
  • Rely, but notify. B101 §3.1.2: the architect is entitled to rely on and is not responsible for the accuracy, completeness and timeliness of information furnished by the owner and the owner’s consultants — but must provide prompt written notice on becoming aware of any error, omission or inconsistency. The reliance protection depends on the notice.
  • Do not act without approval. §3.1.4: the architect is not responsible for an owner directive or substitution, or the owner’s acceptance of non-conforming work, made without the architect’s written approval.
Integrating the owner’s consultants — objective 3.4

Under B101 §5.8 the owner coordinates its own consultants’ services with the architect’s, with the architect’s assistance. The architect may furnish that coordination as a supplemental service (§4.1.1.11, "Architect’s coordination of the Owner’s consultants"). The owner must require its consultants and contractors to maintain insurance, including professional liability, appropriate to their services. And the architect becomes responsible for owner-hired consultants only by agreeing to be, or by certifying their work.

Section 4 · Project Execution 17–23%

PjM 4.1Monitoring compliance with the construction budget

The architect produces an estimate of the Cost of the Work at the end of each design phase — schematic design, design development, construction documents — and updates it, advising the owner of any adjustment. Estimating methods track the information available:

MethodBasisPhaseAccuracy
Area / volume (unit rate)Gross square footage or building volume × a historical cost per unitPre-design, schematic designRoughest — carry a design contingency up to 20%
Elemental / assemblies (UniFormat)Cost by building system — substructure, superstructure, exterior enclosureDesign developmentBetter — systems are known even if products are not
Quantity survey (detailed unit price)Itemised quantities of materials and labour by specification section (MasterFormat)Construction documents; contractor or CM at bidMost accurate

Contingencies fall as design advances: a master-planning contingency commonly starts at 15% and drops to 10% or less; a construction contingency runs generally up to 5%, higher for renovation. An estimator bringing 60% of projects within 5% of the low bid is doing better than expected — statistically one project in five falls outside a 10% range.

The two different over-budget situations

During design (B101 §6.5): if the architect’s estimate exceeds the owner’s budget, the architect makes appropriate recommendations to adjust the project’s size, quality or budget. After bidding (§6.6): if the lowest bona fide bid exceeds the budget, the owner chooses among five options — increase the budget, rebid or renegotiate, terminate, revise the program/scope/quality in consultation with the architect, or another mutually acceptable alternative. Only if the owner picks option 4 does §6.7 require the architect to modify the documents without additional compensation — and that modification is the limit of the architect’s responsibility under Article 6.

Note also the 90-day price-level rule (§6.4): if procurement has not commenced within 90 days after the architect submits the CDs, through no fault of the architect, the budget is adjusted for changes in the general level of prices. The architect is permitted under §6.3 to include contingencies for design, bidding and price escalation in its estimates, to determine what materials and systems are included, to recommend reasonable adjustments to program and scope, and to include design alternates to bring the estimate to the budget.

PjM 4.2Scope changes and scope creep

The three essential contract terms — scope, time frame, fee — are also the three things scope creep attacks. The discipline:

  1. Recognise the change against the written scope. If the request is not in Article 3 and not designated as a supplemental service, it is additional.
  2. Notify the owner with reasonable promptness, explaining the facts and circumstances giving rise to the need.
  3. Obtain written authorisation before proceeding — with an adjustment to both fee and schedule.
  4. Document it. A formal change order or contract amendment signed by all parties is best. If the owner will not sign, confirm in writing and send it anyway, advising that a change has been made to scope, time frame or fee.
Scope creep is rarely one big request

It is a sequence of small favours, each individually cheap. The exam tests the first one: an owner asks for "just one more option" at the end of schematic design. The correct move is not to refuse and not to absorb it silently — it is to tell the owner it is an additional service, price it, and get authorisation. Multiple preliminary designs is on the supplemental services list precisely because it is the most common creep.

PjM 4.4Authorities having jurisdiction

Zoning — what it controls

Use (permitted, conditional, prohibited) · density (units per acre, floor area ratio) · bulk and height · setbacks and yards · lot coverage · parking and loading · signage · sometimes design review in overlay or historic districts.

VarianceRelief from a dimensional requirement — setback, height, coverage — granted on a showing of hardship unique to the property, not self-created, where the relief does not harm the public interest. Heard by a zoning board of appeals.
Special / conditional use permitPermission for a use the ordinance contemplates but conditions — a school in a residential district. Not a hardship showing; a conditions showing.
RezoningA legislative change to the ordinance or the map. The heaviest lift.
Nonconforming useA use lawfully established before the ordinance changed. Generally may continue but may not be expanded, and rights are often lost if the use is abandoned or the structure substantially destroyed.
Administrative remediesMust generally be exhausted — appeals through the board — before a court will hear the matter on judicial review.

Codes

  • The International Code Council (ICC) publishes the I-Codes — IBC, IRC, IEBC, IMC, IPC, IFC, IECC. The NFPA publishes NFPA 101 Life Safety Code and NFPA 70 National Electrical Code. Model codes have no force until adopted by a jurisdiction, usually with amendments — always check the locally adopted edition and its amendments.
  • Prescriptive code provisions state exactly what to do; performance provisions state the outcome to achieve and permit alternative means. Performance paths generally require more analysis and AHJ acceptance.
  • Basic code analysis sequence: occupancy classification → construction type → allowable area and height → fire separation and ratings → means of egress → accessibility → fire protection systems → energy.
  • B101 §3.1.5: the architect contacts governmental authorities required to approve the construction documents and utility providers, and responds to the design requirements they impose. §3.1.6: the architect assists the owner in connection with the owner’s responsibility for filing documents for approval — filing is the owner’s responsibility, the architect assists.
  • Under §4.2.1.2 and .3, services necessitated by the unforeseen enactment or revision of codes, or by official interpretations that contradict earlier AHJ interpretations, are additional services.

Accessibility

The ADA is civil rights legislation, not a building code — it is enforced by the Department of Justice through complaint and litigation, not through a permit review. The U.S. Access Board develops the guidelines; DOJ adopts them as the ADA Standards for Accessible Design. ICC A117.1 is the technical standard referenced by the IBC. Compliance with a locally adopted code does not guarantee ADA compliance — both apply, and the more stringent governs.

Section 5 · Project Quality Control 19–25%

PjM 5.2The four practical QA/QC methods

  1. Internal peer review — an experienced colleague not on the project reviews the documents.
  2. Checklists — standardised, phase-appropriate. AIA D200 Project Checklist lists tasks by phase.
  3. Cross-discipline coordination checks — overlay or model-based clash detection between architectural, structural and MEP.
  4. External review — by owners, contractors, subcontractors, permit agencies, or plan-checking architects and engineers. External reviews should be welcomed, not resisted.
Three rules about review that get tested

1. Specification writers make valuable internal peer reviewers — familiarity with the project helps them coordinate terminology between drawings and specifications and spot where materials or systems are misrepresented. 2. The project manager should always be present when review results are presented — so findings are understood in context and translated into action rather than resented. 3. Peer review is not a substitute for thorough coordination or for a reference set prepared by the project team.

Constructability review

A review of whether the documented design can actually be built as drawn, in the sequence proposed, with available materials and labour, in the site’s conditions. Under CMc and design-build it comes from the constructor during preconstruction — one of the principal benefits of engaging a builder early. Under DBB it must be supplied by the architect’s own experience or a separate consultant, because no contractor is on the team.

Document coordination

The highest-frequency source of claims. What gets checked: drawings against specifications (a material named on the drawing must exist in the spec, and vice versa); architectural against structural against MEP; details against the plans they are keyed from; schedules against plans; the same information appearing in only one place — duplicated information is duplicated opportunity for inconsistency.

The 51% statistic

Communication is the largest single risk driver, and 51% of communication-related claims trace to the lack of a procedure for identifying conflicts, errors and omissions. Not to the conflicts themselves — to the absence of a procedure for finding them. When a question asks what would most reduce a firm’s claims exposure, an answer about establishing a systematic coordination review process beats answers about working harder or hiring better people.

PjM 5.4Maintaining design intent through cost cutting

Objective 5.4. Value engineering, substitutions and scope reduction all threaten design integrity, and the architect’s position is consistent throughout:

  • Evaluate substitutions on the merits — does the proposed product meet the performance criteria, the design intent, and the code requirements? Evaluating substitutions proposed by the owner or contractor, and revising the documents accordingly, is an additional service under B101–2007 §4.3.2.5.
  • Explain consequences, in writing, before the decision. Not "I disagree" but "deleting this will result in X."
  • Recommend; do not decide. The owner owns the trade-off. Once the owner decides, document the decision and proceed — and note §3.1.4: the architect is not responsible for an owner directive or substitution made without the architect’s written approval.
  • Never accept an owner directive that violates code or endangers the public. That is AIA Rule 2.105 territory: advise against it, refuse to consent, and report it to the building official unless it can be resolved otherwise.

Delegated design

Where the contract documents require the contractor to provide professional design services — curtain wall engineering, fire sprinkler layout, precast connections — the architect specifies the performance and design criteria those services must satisfy, reviews submittals that bear the design professional’s seal and signature, and is entitled to rely on and is not responsible for the adequacy and accuracy of those services. Two failure modes the exam likes: specifying no criteria (so there is nothing to review against), and reviewing an unsealed delegated-design submittal.
