Section 4 · Building Analysis & Programming 37–43%

PA 4.1PA 4.3Programming is problem seeking. Design is problem solving.

This is the distinction the section is built on, and Peña states it flatly: "Programming IS analysis. Design IS synthesis." Everything else follows from it. Programming seeks and defines the problem; it does not solve it. If an answer option has the programmer making a physical design decision, it is wrong.

The five stepsEstablish Goals → collect and analyse Facts → uncover and test Concepts → determine Needs → state the Problem. Steps 1–4 can run in any order or simultaneously. Only step 5 must come last. Goals, Concepts and the Problem Statement are qualitative; Facts and Needs are quantitative — they alternate.
StepThe question it asksKind
1 GoalsWhat does the client want to achieve, and why?Qualitative
2 FactsWhat do we know? What is given?Quantitative
3 ConceptsHow does the client want to achieve the goals?Qualitative
4 NeedsHow much money and space? What level of quality?Quantitative — Peña calls this step "a feasibility test"
5 ProblemWhat are the significant conditions affecting the design? What general directions should the design take?Qualitative — "distilling what has been found"

PA 4.3Programmatic concept vs design concept — the most-tested distinction in the section

Peña's own words: "Programmatic concepts refer to abstract ideas intended mainly as functional solutions to clients' performance problems without regard to the physical response. Design concepts refer to concrete ideas intended as physical solutions to clients' architectural problems... programmatic concepts relate to performance problems and design concepts relate to architectural problems."

Programmatic concept (performance)A corresponding design concept (physical)
ConvertibilityA folding door
ShelterA roof
"Decentralise 2,700 students into schools of 900, with four houses in each school"(a) three dispersed buildings, or (b) three floors of one building, or (c) one compact building with three identifiable schools on one floor

That last row is the proof: many design concepts can come from one programmatic concept. The hierarchy is Project Goals (ends) → Programmatic Concepts (means) → Design Concepts (response).

Why the separation matters — Peña's own three reasons

Dealing with design concepts during programming means (1) jumping to conclusions, (2) synthesizing too early, and (3) determining subsolutions before the subproblems were identified. If an option says the programmer should propose a physical form, that is the wrong answer, every time.

Narrative that sticks — the scrapbookA client drops "a big scrapbook full of magazine clippings" on the programmer's desk: a Dutch kitchen, a French Provincial dining room, a Japanese living room, a "Shangri-La porch." Peña: "The scrapbook is the nemesis of the experienced programmer, yet it can be used as a means to seek the problems behind the solutions." That is the whole method in one anecdote — you do not reject the client's images, you reverse-engineer the performance problem behind each one.

PA 4.4Spatial and functional relationships

Peña's own sequence: build an organization chart → issue a questionnaire with proximity codes (critical / desirable / accessible) → transpose into an Interaction Matrix → produce a Bubble Diagram at two scales (Micro: relationships within a group; Macro: the organisation-wide summary). The three governing ideas are Flow, Proximity, and Virtual — Virtual being the exception to the rule that proximity equals communication.

The industry vocabulary Peña does not usePeña never writes "blocking" or "stacking." The sequence the exam expects is: Bubble diagram (relationships, no scale) → Block diagram (accurate areas) → Blocking (assigning departments to areas on a floor) → Stacking (assigning departments to floors). Blocking is horizontal, stacking is vertical. If you can only keep one: stacking has storeys in it.

PA 4.1Net, gross and efficiency — where PA's arithmetic lives

Peña's efficiency model is two factors multiplied: Interior Layout Efficiency × Base Building Efficiency = Overall Building Efficiency. His worked example: 0.61 × 0.84 = 0.51.

Worked example — sizing gross area from a net programme
A corporate office programme totals 61,000 SF of net assignable area. Overall building efficiency for a corporate office (Peña's table) = 0.50 Gross area required = net ÷ efficiency = 61,000 ÷ 0.50 = 122,000 GSF Check it the other way (the grossing-factor form): Grossing factor = 1 ÷ 0.50 = 2.00 Gross = 61,000 × 2.00 = 122,000 GSF ✓
The trap: multiplier or divisor

An efficiency of 0.50 used as a divisor and a grossing factor of 2.00 used as a multiplier give the same answer. Peña prints a conversion table for exactly this reason (1/1.25 = .80 … 1/.50 = 2.0). Items exploit the confusion by offering both 30,500 and 122,000 as options. Decide which direction you are going before you touch the calculator: net → gross always gets bigger.

Correction — two different, both-correct definitions of "gross"

Your JX notes say: "Gross (for programming) = exterior face / Gross (for code) = inside face of exterior walls." That is right, and it is a genuine trap rather than an error. Peña measures Building Gross Area to the outside face of exterior walls. The IBC's gross floor area is measured to the inside perimeter of exterior walls. Reading Problem Seeking alone would leave you with only the programming definition and you would answer an occupant-load item wrong. Keep both, and read the stem for which world you are in.

Correction — "grossing factor" in your Amber Book material

The Panic Notes state "Grossing factor is rentable/usable." That is the R/U ratio (also called the load factor) — a leasing term. In programming, the grossing factor is gross ÷ net, the reciprocal of the efficiency factor, which is how Peña and the exam use it. The workbook's own examples are consistent with the programming meaning (locker room 1.1, classroom 1.5, hospital radiation therapy 1.75), so the Panic Notes sentence contradicts the workbook. Use gross ÷ net.

The four negotiable elements — Peña's feasibility testWhen the programme does not fit the budget, exactly four things can move: (1) space requirements, (2) quality of construction, (3) money budget, (4) time. Peña: "At least one of these four elements must be negotiable." If an item asks what the architect should do when the programme exceeds the budget, the answer names one of these four — never "value engineer the design," which has not been designed yet.

Schedule and programme precision are linked. Peña: "Concurrent (known as fast-track) scheduling requires that some decisions be made sooner, that the money be locked in earlier, that the space program be looser, and that the predictive parameters be shorter and more general." Fast-track buys time and costs precision.

Also in Peña, and directly on objective 4.5: for technical buildings or renovation projects involving building condition assessments, a cost-estimating specialist is often part of the programming team.

PA 4.2Existing buildings

Peña's rule, quotable: "The age of a building is directly proportional to the cost of renovation." His complete list of renovation cost drivers: prior occupancy; floor-to-floor height; mechanical, electrical, and plumbing systems; energy efficiency; structural capacities; seismic codes; and life safety and disabilities access guidelines. And the directive: "Always compare major renovation to new construction, even if it is desirable to salvage the building for historical purposes... base a reliable renovation cost estimate on a building condition assessment."

The four Secretary of the Interior's treatments, in increasing order of intervention: Preservation (retain as found) → Rehabilitation (alter for a compatible new use, retaining character) → Restoration (return to a specific period, removing later work) → Reconstruction (rebuild what is gone). A property must generally be 50 years old to be eligible for the National Register. Verified

Correction — why historic repointing uses soft mortar

Your JX notes give the rationale as "to limit brick expansion and contraction from thermal changes." That is the wrong reason. The mortar must be softer than the historic masonry unit so that the joint — which is repairable — is the sacrificial element that cracks and erodes, rather than the irreplaceable brick or stone. A hard modern mortar around a soft old brick destroys the brick. The conclusion (use Type O, high-lime) is right; the reasoning in your notes is not, and the exam tests the reasoning.

Contours — the only rule you need

Contour readingContours point uphill at a valley or swale; they point downhill at a ridge or crown. Concentric closed loops increasing toward the centre = a hill; decreasing toward the centre = a depression. Closely spaced = steep; widely spaced = flat. Contours never cross except at an overhang or cliff. Slope % = (vertical rise ÷ horizontal run) × 100.
Worked example — buildable area on a sloped site
Site: 300 ft × 200 ft = 60,000 SF (1.377 acres) Zoning: front setback 25 ft, rear 20 ft, sides 10 ft each Contours: the rear 60 ft of the lot falls at 18% slope Step 1 — envelope after setbacks Buildable depth = 200 − 25 − 20 = 155 ft Buildable width = 300 − 10 − 10 = 280 ft Setback envelope = 155 × 280 = 43,400 SF Step 2 — remove the unbuildable slope Rear 60 ft is at 18%. Only 40 ft of that strip sits inside the rear setback line, so 40 ft × 280 ft = 11,200 SF is steep ground inside the envelope. The floodplain trap

A "100-year floodplain" is a 1% annual chance of flooding in any given year — not "it floods once a century." Your JX notes get this right, and it is worth keeping: the 25-year floodplain is a 1-in-25 (4%) chance this year. The 500-year floodplain is the 0.2% annual chance zone. The same site can flood twice in five years without contradicting anything.

The same notes also list a "Geologic (100,000-year floodplain)." That is not FEMA/NFIP terminology and should not be treated as an exam-testable category.

Worked example — FAR, coverage and height together
Lot: 150 ft × 200 ft = 30,000 SF Zoning: FAR 2.5 max · lot coverage 50% max · height 65 ft max Floor-to-floor assumed 13 ft Maximum floor area = 2.5 × 30,000 = 75,000 SF Maximum footprint = 0.50 × 30,000 = 15,000 SF Storeys needed = 75,000 ÷ 15,000 = 5 storeys Height check = 5 × 13 ft = 65 ft 65 ≤ 65 → just fits. Now change one variable: the client wants 14 ft floor-to-floor. 5 × 14 = 70 ft > 65 ft limit. At 14 ft you get 65 ÷ 14 = 4.64 → 4 full storeys. 4 × 15,000 = 60,000 SF — you lose 15,000 SF of FAR you are legally entitled to. This is the tested move: the height limit, not the FAR, becomes the binding constraint the moment floor-to-floor grows.
If two rules conflict, the more restrictive governs

Zoning and the building code are separate bodies of law and neither defers to the other. Where zoning allows 65 ft and the building code's construction type allows 55 ft, you get 55 ft. Where the code would allow 5 storeys and zoning allows 3, you get 3. Always the lower. Your Jenny's Notes state this correctly.

Note on azimuth in your Panic Notes

They define azimuth as "the angle of the sun relative to due south." Azimuth is more commonly measured clockwise from due north. Both conventions exist and the exam is unlikely to hinge on it, but if a diagram gives you an azimuth, read its own north arrow rather than assuming.

PA 1.2Soils and what they cost you

Particle size, largest to smallest: boulders → cobbles → gravel → sand → silt → clay. Bearing competence follows roughly the same order: gravel > sand > silt > clay. Gravel and sand are frictional; clay is cohesive; silt can be either. Organic soils — peat and topsoil — cannot be built on.

Atterberg limits: as water is added to dry soil it passes the plastic limit (soil becomes mouldable) and then the liquid limit (soil flows). The plasticity index is the difference between them; PI greater than 15 indicates expansive soil. Verified, Site Planning Handbook

Presumptive bearing values are on the Numbers page, from IBC Table 1806.2. Angle of repose is the steepest angle an unsupported soil face will hold before sliding.

PA 1.3Onsite resources

  • Solar — orientation and unobstructed south exposure; PV tilt equal to latitude for a year-round optimum, latitude −15° favouring summer, latitude +15° favouring winter.
  • Wind — for ventilation, and for generation where speeds justify it. Turbine power varies with the cube of wind speed and the square of rotor diameter.
  • Water — rainwater capture, and greywater, which under the plumbing code may not be stored untreated for more than 24 hours. Verified
  • Ground — geothermal exchange: vertical wells where land is tight, horizontal trenches where it is not.
  • Existing structures and materials — reuse avoids the embodied carbon already spent.
  • Topography — earth-sheltering, gravity drainage, cut-and-fill balance.
  • Vegetation — existing mature trees for shade and windbreak; they cannot be bought back.

A stormwater pollution prevention plan (SWPPP) is required under the EPA's construction general permit for land disturbance of one acre or more. Verified One acre = 43,560 SF.
