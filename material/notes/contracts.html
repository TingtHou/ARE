The contract documents, article by article

If you learn one thing to exam standard, make it B101. Its article order is a mental filing cabinet: once you can say what lives in Article 4 versus Article 6, most contract questions answer themselves.

This deck teaches the unmodified AIA text

Everything below is the AIA standard form — B101–2017 unless a clause is flagged as 2007, A101–2007, and A201–2017. That is what NCARB writes items against. Owners modify these documents constantly, and the copies that circulate online are often somebody’s marked-up version, so when you read a real agreement, check it against the standard before you trust a clause number or a day count. A short note on how to spot a modified agreement is at the end of this page.

§2.5 — the insurance the architect must carry

A genuine 2007→2017 change. B101–2007 §2.5 was essentially a blank: it told the parties to identify types and limits, and added that if the owner’s requirements exceed what the architect normally carries, the owner reimburses the additional cost. B101–2017 §2.5 enumerates the coverages, each with a blank for the limit:

  • Commercial General Liability — bodily injury and property damage
  • Automobile Liability — owned, non-owned and hired vehicles
  • Workers’ Compensation at statutory limits, and Employers’ Liability
  • Umbrella or Excess Liability
  • Professional Liability covering negligent acts, errors and omissions
The limits are blanks — the list is not

Learn the five coverages §2.5 enumerates, not any dollar figure. Any specific limit you see in a real agreement was typed in by that owner. The one number-adjacent rule worth carrying: if the owner requires coverage beyond what the architect normally maintains, the owner reimburses the additional cost — and the consultants’ equivalent expense is a reimbursable expense under §11.8.1.

The phase-deliverable trap

Items are often written to test whether you put a deliverable in the right phase. Outline specifications = Design Development. Full specifications = Construction Documents. Study models and perspective sketches = Schematic Design. A site plan appears in SD "if appropriate." Addenda are issued in Procurement, never in CD. A Construction Change Directive belongs to Construction.

PjM 3.3Article 3.6 — Construction Phase, in detail

When it starts and stops

Commences with the award of the Contract for Construction and terminates on the date the architect issues the final Certificate for Payment. Not at substantial completion — at final payment.

Evaluations of the work (§3.6.2)

The architect visits the site at intervals appropriate to the stage of construction to become generally familiar with progress and quality, and to determine in general if the work observed will be in accordance with the contract documents when completed. The architect is not required to make exhaustive or continuous on-site inspections.

The four words that decide most CA questions

Visit, not inspect. Generally familiar, not thoroughly informed. In general, not in detail. Observed, not discovered. If an answer choice has the architect guaranteeing, warranting, inspecting exhaustively, or controlling means and methods, it is wrong. The architect has the authority to reject Work that does not conform to the Contract Documents (§3.6.2.2), and may recommend additional inspection or testing — but exercising or not exercising that authority creates no duty to the contractor or subcontractors.

Certificates for Payment (§3.6.3)

A certificate is a representation to the owner, based on site evaluations and the data in the application, that to the best of the architect’s knowledge, information and belief the work has progressed to the point indicated, the quality is in accordance with the contract documents, and the contractor is entitled to the amount certified.

It is not a representation that the architect has (1) made exhaustive or continuous on-site inspections, (2) reviewed construction means, methods, techniques, sequences or procedures, (3) reviewed copies of subcontractor requisitions, or (4) ascertained how the contractor used money previously paid. Memorise those four negatives.

Submittals (§3.6.4)

Review is only for the limited purpose of checking conformance with the information given and the design concept expressed in the contract documents. It is not for determining accuracy and completeness of dimensions, quantities, or installation and performance of equipment — those are the contractor’s. Review does not approve safety precautions or means and methods. Approval of an item is not approval of the assembly of which it is a component.

Where the contract documents require the contractor to provide delegated design — professional services or certifications by a design professional — the architect specifies the performance and design criteria, reviews submittals bearing that professional’s seal and signature, and is entitled to rely on and is not responsible for the adequacy of those services.

Changes in the Work (§3.6.5)

InstrumentWho signsChanges contract sum or time?When used
Minor change in the workArchitect aloneNo — neitherConsistent with the intent of the contract documents. The architect may simply order it.
Construction Change Directive (CCD)Owner and Architect (not the contractor)Yes — directs a change with an interim or disputed adjustmentOwner wants the change now; parties have not yet agreed on price or time. Work proceeds; the amount is settled later.

Signature count is the whole question

One signature = minor change. Two = CCD. Three = change order. A CCD frequently becomes a change order once the contractor agrees to the adjustment. If a question says the contractor disagrees with the cost but the owner needs the work to proceed, the answer is a Construction Change Directive.

Project completion (§3.6.6)

  1. Conduct inspections to determine the date(s) of Substantial Completion and the date of final completion. (Note the word shift: the architect visits during construction but inspects at completion.)
  2. Issue Certificates of Substantial Completion (AIA G704).
  3. Forward written warranties and related documents received from the contractor to the owner.
  4. Issue a final Certificate for Payment based on a final inspection.

The architect also verifies the accuracy and completeness of the contractor’s list of work to be completed or corrected — the punch list is prepared by the contractor, then checked by the architect. And at substantial completion the architect informs the owner of the balance of the contract sum remaining, including any amount to be retained.

Substantial CompletionThe stage when the work is sufficiently complete so the owner can occupy or use it for its intended purpose. Triggers: owner occupancy, start of warranties and the one-year correction period, release of most retainage, transfer of insurance and utilities responsibility, and the end of liquidated damages.
Final CompletionAll work including the punch list is finished. Triggers final payment and, generally, the waiver of most claims by both parties.
One-year correction periodNot a warranty period — it is the contractor’s obligation to correct nonconforming work discovered within one year of substantial completion. Warranties may be longer or shorter.
Post-occupancy meetingB101 §3.6.6.5: upon the owner’s request and before one year from substantial completion, the architect reviews facility operation and performance at no additional compensation.

PjM 3.1Article 4 — basic vs. supplemental vs. additional

This distinction generates more PjM items than any other single idea. Three buckets:

Basic Services

Article 3. Usual and customary, including structural, mechanical and electrical engineering. Included in the base fee.

Supplemental Services

§4.1. Known at signing, listed in a table, assigned to architect or owner. If neither is designated, it is not being provided at all.

Additional Services

§4.2. Arise later. Require written authorisation before proceeding. Compensated separately with a schedule adjustment.

The §4.1 supplemental services list

Programming · multiple preliminary designs · measured drawings · existing facilities surveys · site evaluation and planning · Building Information Modeling · as-constructed record drawings · post-occupancy evaluation · facility support services · tenant-related services · architect’s coordination of the owner’s consultants · telecommunications/data design · security evaluation · commissioning · fast-track design services · historic preservation · furniture, furnishings and equipment design · other specialty consultant services.

Programming is not a basic service

The single most reliable supplemental-services item. Programming, existing-conditions surveys, BIM, record drawings, post-occupancy evaluation, commissioning, FF&E and LEED certification work are all outside Basic Services. If a client asks the architect to write the program, that is either a supplemental service priced at signing or an additional service requiring written authorisation.

The §4.2.1 additional services triggers — written authorisation required first

  1. A change in Initial Information, previous instructions or approvals, or a material change in the project — size, quality, complexity, the owner’s schedule or budget, or the procurement or delivery method.
  2. The unforeseen enactment or revision of codes, laws or regulations.
  3. Editing Instruments of Service because of official code interpretations that contradict earlier AHJ interpretations, or that contradict documents prepared within the standard of care.
  4. The owner’s failure to decide in a timely manner, or any other failure of performance by the owner, the owner’s consultants or the owner’s contractors.
  5. Preparing digital models for transmission to the owner’s consultants and contractors.
  6. Design and documentation for alternate bid or proposal requests proposed by the owner.
  7. Preparation for and attendance at a dispute resolution or legal proceeding — except where the architect is a party.
  8. Consultation concerning replacement of work resulting from fire or other cause during construction.

B101–2007 adds three more that the 2017 edition folds in or drops: extensive environmentally responsible design alternatives or LEED certification; public presentations, meetings or hearings; evaluating the qualifications of bidders; and assistance to the Initial Decision Maker if other than the architect.

§4.3.2 (2007) — services the architect provides, then notifies

A distinct category worth knowing: to avoid delay in the construction phase, the architect provides these and notifies the owner promptly rather than waiting for authorisation — and the owner may then say it did not want them.

  • Reviewing a submittal out of sequence from the agreed submittal schedule.
  • Responding to an improper RFI — one not prepared in accordance with the contract documents, or seeking information the contractor could get from a careful study of the documents, field conditions and prior correspondence.
  • Preparing change orders and CCDs that require evaluation of the contractor’s proposals and supporting data.
  • Evaluating an extensive number of claims as Initial Decision Maker.
  • Evaluating substitutions proposed by the owner or contractor and revising the documents accordingly.
  • Providing construction phase services more than 60 days after substantial completion (2007) — the 2017 edition moves this to 90 days.

§4.2.3 — the numeric CA limits

These are blanks in the standard form — the parties negotiate and fill in each number. Once a limit is reached the architect notifies the owner, and further service of that kind is an additional service. Typical filled values run to two or three reviews of each submittal and one inspection each for substantial and final completion, but no number is standard: if an exam item gives you a count, it is telling you what that agreement says, not what B101 says.

PjM 3.4Article 5 — Owner’s Responsibilities

The owner has fewer detailed duties than the architect, because the owner’s job is narrower: provide information, make decisions, and pay. But they are real obligations, and they matter most when something goes wrong. Note your instructor’s framing: because these are AIA forms, they impose more express obligations on an owner than many owner-drafted agreements do — which is exactly why an owner’s counsel marks them up.

§5.1 Timely informationThe owner provides information in a timely manner regarding project requirements and limitations, including a written program. This is tied directly to the schedule: if the owner fails to decide or to furnish information on time and the architect’s work is delayed, that delay should not automatically become the architect’s responsibility — and under §4.2.1.4 the resulting services are an additional service.
§5.2 BudgetThe owner establishes and periodically updates the budget for the Project — the Cost of the Work, the owner’s other costs, and reasonable contingencies for all of them. If the owner significantly increases or decreases the budget, the owner shall notify the architect, and the parties agree a corresponding change in scope and quality.
§5.3 RepresentativeThe owner identifies a representative authorised to act on the owner’s behalf, and renders decisions and approvals in a timely manner to avoid unreasonable delay. Communication should move through people with authority to bind their party — that is the whole point of the clause.
§5.4–5.5 Site informationThe owner furnishes surveys (grades, streets, rights-of-way, restrictions, easements, encroachments, zoning, deed restrictions, boundaries, contours, existing improvements, utilities) and the services of geotechnical engineers — borings, test pits, soil bearing values, percolation tests, hazardous-materials evaluation.
§5.9–5.10 Tests and professionalsThe owner furnishes tests, inspections and reports required by law or the contract documents, and all legal, insurance and accounting services, including auditing.
§5.11 Notice of defectsThe owner gives prompt written notice to the architect if it becomes aware of any fault or defect, including errors, omissions or inconsistencies in the architect’s Instruments of Service.
§5.13 The construction contractBefore executing it, the owner coordinates the architect’s duties under the Contract for Construction with the architect’s services under B101, and provides the architect a copy of the executed agreement, including the General Conditions.
§5.15 Lien information — 15 daysWithin 15 days of a written request from the architect, the owner furnishes the information necessary and relevant for the architect to evaluate, give notice of, or enforce lien rights.

Mechanic’s liens — why §5.15 exists

Your instructor spent real time here, and it is worth knowing because it explains an otherwise odd clause. Every state has a statutory mechanism allowing persons who supply labour, materials or professional services that improve real property to assert a lien against that property if they are not paid. Architects are covered in most jurisdictions.

  • Why it is powerful: an ordinary breach-of-contract claim gets you a judgment — and then you still have to collect. A mechanic’s lien attaches the unpaid claim to the improved property itself. If the statutory requirements are met, the claimant may be able to foreclose and force a sale of the property to get paid.
  • Priority: a perfected construction lien may have priority over other property interests, potentially including a mortgage, depending on the governing law. That is why lenders care about them.
  • The catch: because the remedy is severe, claimants must strictly comply with notice and filing deadlines. Miss a statutory deadline and the lien remedy is gone — though the conventional breach-of-contract claim usually survives.
  • Deadlines and details vary by jurisdiction. Always check the applicable state statute; there is no national rule.
  • The B101 connection: to evaluate or preserve a lien you need ownership and property information you may not have. §5.15 obliges the owner to hand it over within 15 days of a written request. B101 §8.2.1 also lets the architect proceed to comply with lien notice or filing deadlines before the dispute is resolved by mediation — the one carve-out from mediation-as-condition-precedent.
The lien exception to mediation

Mediation is a condition precedent to binding dispute resolution — except that where the matter relates to or is the subject of a lien arising out of the architect’s services, the architect may proceed in accordance with applicable law to comply with the lien notice or filing deadlines first. The reason is exactly the point above: lien deadlines are unforgiving, and mediation takes time.

PjM 4.1Article 6 — Cost of the Work

Cost of the Work includesThe total cost to the owner to construct all elements designed or specified by the architect, including the contractor’s general conditions costs, overhead and profit, plus the reasonable value of labour, materials and equipment donated to or furnished by the owner.
Cost of the Work excludesThe architect’s compensation; the cost of land, rights-of-way and financing; contingencies for changes in the work; and other costs that are the owner’s responsibility.
Project BudgetBroader: Cost of the Work plus all architect and consultant fees, other owner fees and costs, equipment and FF&E, and contingencies. Cost of the Work ≠ Project Budget.
Estimates are judgment, not warranty§6.2: neither party controls the cost of labour, materials or equipment, the contractor’s methods of determining bid prices, or market conditions. The architect does not warrant that bids will not vary from the estimate.
The 90-day rule§6.4: if procurement has not commenced within 90 days after the architect submits the construction documents, through no fault of the architect, the owner’s budget is adjusted for changes in the general level of prices.

§6.6 — when the lowest bona fide bid exceeds the budget, the OWNER shall:

  1. Increase the budget for the Cost of the Work;
  2. Authorise rebidding or renegotiating within a reasonable time;
  3. Terminate in accordance with §9.5;
  4. In consultation with the architect, revise the program, scope or quality to reduce the Cost of the Work; or
  5. Implement any other mutually acceptable alternative.
§6.7 — the redesign-for-free clause

If the owner chooses option 4 (revise scope), the architect shall modify the construction documents to comply with the budget — without additional compensation. That modification is the limit of the architect’s responsibility under Article 6. This is the most commonly tested consequence in the whole document: the architect redesigns once, for free, and is then done. It is not a guarantee of construction cost, and it is not an unlimited obligation to keep redesigning.

Whose fault was the overage? — the instructor’s two scenarios

§6.7 is not a blanket rule that the architect always redesigns for free. Fault matters, and your instructor illustrated it with a pair worth memorising.

Scenario A — architect’s fault. The architect has repeatedly represented the project will cost about $1,000,000. It bids at $1,400,000, and the overage traces to the architect’s failure to pay attention to the agreed budget. The architect redesigns without additional compensation.

Scenario B — owner’s fault. The owner’s stated budget was $1,000,000, but the owner quietly cut it to $750,000 and never told the architect. Bids come in at $950,000 — within the budget the architect was designing to, but above the owner’s new one. Redesign caused by the owner’s uncommunicated change is an additional service, compensable under §4.2.1.1 (a material change in the owner’s budget for the Cost of the Work).

The principle: the obligation runs on the flow of current information in both directions. The owner communicates the budget and any change to it (§5.2); the architect estimates the Cost of the Work at each phase and advises of adjustments (§3.2.6, §3.3.2, §3.4.4). Whoever broke the information flow bears the redesign.

Why contracts state things that seem obvious

A point worth carrying into every contract question. Many provisions look like common sense — “the owner shall provide information in a timely manner.” Their value is that they make the rules explicit, so that when a dispute arises the parties can point to written responsibilities instead of relying on differing memories and assumptions. If an answer choice turns on what someone remembered agreeing to, it is usually the wrong answer; the one that turns on what the agreement says is usually right.

PjM 2.3Article 7 — Copyrights and Licenses

  • The architect and its consultants are the authors and owners of their respective Instruments of Service and retain all common-law, statutory and other reserved rights including copyrights.
  • The owner receives a nonexclusive licence to use them solely and exclusively for constructing, using, maintaining, altering and adding to this Project — conditioned on the owner substantially performing its obligations, including prompt payment.
  • The licence permits the owner to authorise the contractor, subcontractors, suppliers and its own consultants to reproduce applicable portions for this project only.
  • If the architect rightfully terminates for cause, the licence terminates.
  • If the owner uses the Instruments of Service without retaining their author, the owner releases and indemnifies the architect from claims arising from that use — unless the owner rightfully terminated for cause.
  • Submitting documents to meet regulatory requirements is not publication and does not forfeit reserved rights.
Why this article exists at all

Your instructor’s framing is the one to carry into the exam. Architects do not manufacture a product. They provide professional services — and the principal tangible output of those services is a set of drawings. Site visits, meetings, observation and professional judgment cannot be duplicated. Drawings can be copied instantly, especially in electronic form. So protecting the documents is protecting the livelihood and the authorship both.

Case study — the interlocking circular house

A well-known Chicago-area architect designed a modern house on the North Shore, organised as a series of interlocking circular spaces. High-end work, substantial fees. Tension developed with the client, and the relationship ended before the construction documents were complete. The agreement said the architect owned the copyright in the drawings. The owner had received drawings embodying the design, fired the architect, and did not pay the remaining fees. Construction had not begun, though the central design concept was developed.

The owner gave the drawings and the design concept to a different architect, who completed the documents. The house was built, won awards, and appeared on the cover of a local architectural publication — under the new architect’s name.

The harmScale
Unpaid balance for work already doneComparatively small — perhaps ten or twenty thousand dollars, not enough to justify litigation when the relationship ended
Lost fees the architect would have earnedSubstantially more — remaining design fees, construction-document services, construction-phase services
Lost authorshipThe largest loss. Another architect received the public credit, the awards and the magazine coverage
What the architect had to prove — and the lesson

To make out the copyright claim the original architect had to (1) establish ownership of a protected copyright, (2) show that no broad licence had been transferred, and (3) demonstrate that the built house used protected aspects of the earlier design — while answering the owner’s argument that it had participated in developing the concept and had implied or contractual permission to use the drawings.

The exam lesson: the licence in §7.3 is conditioned on the owner substantially performing its obligations, including prompt payment, and terminates if the architect rightfully terminates for cause. Termination for cause and termination for convenience are treated differently — but neither automatically transfers ownership of the copyright. The practical lesson: the unpaid invoice is rarely the real loss. Authorship and recognition carry commercial and professional value that a small fee dispute can obscure until it is too late.

Ownership of the paper vs. ownership of the copyright

Two separate things. Handing over a drawing set does not transfer copyright. The U.S. Copyright Act protects original works of authorship fixed in a tangible medium — for architects, the drawings, models and the built structure itself — but not procedures, processes or systems, which are patent matters. Copyrights register with the U.S. Copyright Office; trademarks and patents with the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office.

PjM 3.3Article 8 — Claims and Disputes

  1. Mutual waiver of consequential damages (§8.1.3, 2007). Applies without limitation to damages from either party’s termination. Direct damages — the cost of ripping out and rebuilding the noncompliant stair — are still recoverable; consequential damages — the owner’s lost rent because the building opened late — are waived.
  2. Waiver of subrogation to the extent covered by property insurance (§8.1.2). Each party waives rights against the other and against the other’s contractors, consultants, agents and employees, except as to insurance proceeds.
  3. Mediation is a condition precedent to binding dispute resolution (§8.2.1). Administered by the AAA under its Construction Industry Mediation Rules. Parties share the mediator’s fee and filing fees equally; held where the project is located. A filed binding proceeding is stayed 60 days pending mediation.
  4. Binding dispute resolution (§8.2.4) — a checkbox: arbitration, litigation, or other. If nothing is checked and nothing is later agreed in writing, the dispute goes to litigation. That default is a favourite exam item.
  5. The 10-year outer limit (§8.1.1): all claims and causes of action must be commenced in accordance with the selected method of binding dispute resolution and within the period specified by applicable law, but in any case not more than 10 years after the date of Substantial Completion of the Work. Claims not commenced in accordance with §8.1.1 are waived. Both the 2007 and 2017 editions carry this 10-year cap — it is a contractual outer bar sitting on top of whatever the state statutes of limitation and repose already provide.

PjM 2.3Article 9 — Termination or Suspension: the notice periods

Owner fails to paySubstantial nonperformance. The architect may terminate, or suspend after 7 days’ written notice. No liability to the owner for delay caused by that suspension. Before resuming, the owner pays all sums due plus the expenses of interruption and resumption; fees and schedule are equitably adjusted.
Owner suspends the projectThe architect is compensated for services performed before the notice, and for the expenses of interruption and resumption when work restarts.
Suspension exceeds 90 cumulative daysThrough no fault of the architect — the architect may terminate on not less than 7 days’ written notice.
Termination for causeEither party, on not less than 7 days’ written notice, if the other fails substantially to perform through no fault of the terminating party.
Termination for convenienceThe owner only, on not less than 7 days’ written notice, without cause.
What the architect is paidServices performed, reimbursable expenses incurred, and termination expenses — which in the 2007 form are defined as costs directly attributable to termination plus an amount for the architect’s anticipated profit on unperformed services.
Seven days, and everything is in writing

Every notice period in B101 Article 9 is seven days. If an option offers 10, 14 or 30 days for a B101 termination or suspension, it is wrong. And note the asymmetry: only the owner may terminate for convenience. The architect’s route out is either the owner’s substantial nonperformance or a suspension exceeding 90 cumulative days.

PjM 2.3Article 11 — Compensation and reimbursables

Reimbursable expenses (B101–2007 §11.8.1) — the eleven categories

Transportation and authorised out-of-town travel and subsistence · long-distance and dedicated data/communication services, project websites and extranets · fees paid for securing AHJ approvals · printing, reproductions, plots, standard form documents · postage, handling and delivery · overtime authorised in advance · renderings, models, mock-ups, professional photography and presentation materials requested by the owner · the consultants’ expense of project-specific professional liability insurance or coverage in excess of what they normally carry · all taxes levied on professional services and on reimbursables · site office expenses · other similar project-related expenditures.

Reimbursables are billed at cost plus a markup — the handbook gives approximately 10% as typical. Where a not-to-exceed limit is imposed, the architect needs a reasonable estimate with a contingency built in.

Payments

  • An initial payment is made on execution and is the minimum payment under the agreement; it is credited to the owner’s account in the final invoice.
  • Payments are made monthly in proportion to services performed, due and payable on presentation of the invoice.
  • Unpaid amounts bear interest after the stated number of days, or at the legal rate at the architect’s principal place of business.
  • The owner may not withhold from the architect’s compensation to impose a penalty or liquidated damages, or to offset sums paid to contractors for changes in the work — unless the architect agrees or has been found liable in a binding dispute resolution proceeding.

Article 5 — how a progress payment is computed

  1. Take the portion of the contract sum properly allocable to completed work — percentage complete of each portion × that portion’s share of the contract sum per the schedule of values — less retainage.
  2. Add the portion allocable to materials and equipment delivered and suitably stored at the site (or off site if approved in advance in writing), less retainage.
  3. Subtract the aggregate of previous payments.
  4. Subtract amounts for which the architect has withheld or nullified a Certificate for Payment.

At substantial completion the payment is increased to the full contract sum less amounts the architect determines for incomplete work, retainage applicable to that work, and unsettled claims. Final payment is due no later than 30 days after the architect issues the final Certificate for Payment, and requires that the contractor has fully performed and that a final certificate has been issued.

Schedule of valuesAllocates the entire contract sum among portions of the work, in a form and with supporting data the architect may require. Unless the architect objects, it becomes the basis for reviewing applications for payment. The contractor prepares it; the architect reviews it.
RetainageA percentage withheld from each progress payment as security for completion. Generally released at or after substantial completion, often with consent of surety.
AlternatesPriced options the owner may accept, typically at bid. Listed in §4.2 with the date each price expires.
Unit pricesA stated price per unit of measure, with quantity limitations, used when quantities are not yet known.
AllowancesA sum carried in the contract sum for items not yet selected. The contractor includes the allowance; the difference is reconciled by change order once the actual item is chosen.
Initial Decision MakerThe architect by default. Renders an initial decision on claims as a condition precedent to mediation. If someone else is appointed, the architect’s assistance to that person is an additional service under B101–2007 §4.3.1.11.

PjM 3.3The A-series pricing models — A101, A102, A103

Your instructor framed the construction side as two documents, always: a front-end agreement that identifies the parties and sets the business model — price, scope, schedule, payment — and the A201 General Conditions, which supply the operating rules. The front-end agreement is A101, A102 or A103, and the only thing that changes between them is how the contractor gets paid.

DocumentBasis of paymentHow it worksOwner’s exposure
A101Stipulated sum (lump sum)The contractor reviews the drawings, bids, and commits to build for a fixed amount — “I can build this for ten million dollars.”Capped, but opaque. The owner pays the bid whether the contractor’s real cost was $10M or $8M. No visibility into actual cost.
A102Cost of the Work plus a fee, WITH a Guaranteed Maximum PriceThe contractor is reimbursed actual costs — labour, subcontractors, vendors, materials — plus a negotiated fee, usually a percentage; and the total is capped by a GMP.Capped and transparent. The owner sees the real bills and is protected by the ceiling. If the allowable cost comes in under the GMP, the owner does not automatically pay the full amount.
A103Cost of the Work plus a fee, WITHOUT a Guaranteed Maximum PriceThe same cost-plus reimbursement — but no ceiling.Transparent but uncapped. Your instructor’s phrase for this exposure was “an open checkbook”: without a cap, the contractor has no strong contractual incentive to control the total.
A104Abbreviated agreementFor projects of limited scope; incorporates its own condensed general conditions.—
Correction — your lecture transcript reverses A102 and A103

The transcript states that A102 is the uncapped cost-plus form and A103 adds the guaranteed maximum price. That is backwards. The AIA numbering is: A102 = Cost of the Work Plus a Fee with a Guaranteed Maximum Price; A103 = Cost of the Work Plus a Fee without a Guaranteed Maximum Price. Either the instructor misspoke or the transcription garbled it — but NCARB will test the AIA numbering, so learn it the AIA way.

A mnemonic that survives the confusion: the lower number is the safer contract. A101 fixed price, A102 capped cost-plus, A103 uncapped cost-plus — owner risk rises as the number rises. The same logic runs through the CM family: A133 has the GMP option, A134 has no predetermined GMP.

Why an owner might prefer a GMP on a large project

Your instructor’s argument: under a pure lump sum, a contractor might bid $10M on work whose actual cost turns out to be $8M, and the owner never sees the difference. On a large project that gap can be very large. A GMP gives the owner both cost visibility and a ceiling — “the best features of both systems.” But large projects are still routinely delivered lump-sum: competitive bidding requirements, government procurement rules and other project-specific factors may make lump sum appropriate or legally mandatory. He noted having worked on projects worth hundreds of millions structured as lump-sum agreements.

PjM 3.3A201 — the General Conditions

Roughly 45 pages, and among the most widely used documents in the construction industry. Your instructor called it “the guts” of the construction agreement — the rules of the game, as against the front-end agreement’s business terms.

Front-end agreement (A101/A102/A103)A201 General Conditions

Why the architect must know a document it never signs

The architect is not a party to the owner–contractor agreement and does not sign A201 — but A201 repeatedly assigns the architect duties: interpreting the Contract Documents, reviewing submittals, certifying payment, acting as Initial Decision Maker, evaluating claims. So the architect’s obligations arise from two documents at once: B101 and A201. B101 §10.2 expressly incorporates A201 by reference. Read them together or you will not know your own job.

There is a real tension the instructor flagged: A201 describes the architect as neutral in certain decision-making functions, even though the architect has a contractual relationship with the owner. AIA Rule 3.202 says the same thing — when acting as independent interpreter of the documents and judge of contract performance, render decisions impartially.

A201 Article 1 — the definition of “Contract Documents”

Article 1 opens with definitions, and this one matters more than it looks: the defined term determines which documents have binding legal effect on the owner and contractor. Capitalised, it is not a generic label — it is a legal definition establishing the governing package.

Included

The owner–contractor agreement · the Conditions of the Contract (General, Supplementary and other) · Drawings · Specifications · Addenda issued before execution · Modifications issued after execution (change orders, CCDs, minor changes, written amendments) · other documents specifically enumerated in the agreement.

Excluded unless enumerated

The advertisement or invitation to bid · instructions to bidders · sample forms · the contractor’s bid · background information furnished for bidding — experience, project team, qualifications, criminal history. Useful for selecting a contractor; not part of the contract.
The practical consequence — the instructor’s best point

If the construction schedule is not listed or incorporated as a Contract Document, the owner may have difficulty asserting a contract claim based on a missed schedule. The contractor will argue the schedule was informational, not contractually binding. The same trap catches the schedule of values and other project-management documents.

So: the parties must identify, in Article 9 of A101, every document they intend to have binding legal effect. A construction lawyer reviewing a non-AIA form routinely amends that list to add the schedule, the schedule of values, or other instruments the owner is relying on. On the exam, the question usually arrives as “the owner wants to hold the contractor to X — is X enforceable?” and the answer turns on whether X was enumerated.

Something to actually do in practice

Get the executed owner–contractor contract, including all amendments. Because the architect may be asked to interpret the agreement and act as Initial Decision Maker, it needs to know whether provisions have been modified and precisely which documents form the rules of the game. B101 §5.13 obliges the owner to provide it: “the Owner shall provide the Architect a copy of the executed agreement between the Owner and Contractor, including the General Conditions.” Ask for it, and read the Supplementary Conditions.

The shape of the document — how to hold it in your head

Articles 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 answer who does what — one article per party. Articles 7, 8 and 9 answer how the deal changes and how money moves — changes, time, payment. Articles 10 through 14 are the operational and exit provisions. Article 15 is what happens when it all goes wrong. If you can place a question in one of those four groups you have usually found the answer.

Article 3 — the contractor’s duties an architect must know

  • §3.2.2 Review of the documents. The contractor shall carefully study and compare the Contract Documents with each other and with field conditions and report errors, inconsistencies and omissions to the architect. Crucially: the contractor is NOT required to ascertain that the documents comply with applicable laws and codes — but if it becomes aware of a nonconformity, it must promptly report it. The contractor is liable only to the extent of its failure to report something it actually discovered, not for the underlying design error.
  • §3.3.1 Means and methods. The contractor is solely responsible for and in control of construction means, methods, techniques, sequences and procedures. This is the clause behind every exam answer that strips the architect of means-and-methods responsibility.
  • §3.4.2 Substitutions. The contractor may substitute a material or product only with the consent of the owner, after evaluation by the architect, and by Change Order or Construction Change Directive. A substitution is never a unilateral contractor decision.
  • §3.5 Warranty. The contractor warrants that materials and equipment will be new and of good quality, that the Work will be free from defects not inherent in the quality required, and that it will conform to the Contract Documents. The warranty excludes damage or defect caused by abuse, alterations not executed by the contractor, improper or insufficient maintenance or operation, or normal wear and tear.
  • §3.7.1 Permits. The contractor secures and pays for the building permit and other permits, fees, licences and inspections necessary for proper execution of the Work.
  • §3.7.4 Concealed or unknown conditions. On encountering subsurface or concealed physical conditions that differ materially from those indicated, or unknown conditions of an unusual nature, the contractor gives notice before the conditions are disturbed and in no event later than 14 days after first observance. 2007 said 21 days; 2017 shortened it to 14.
  • §3.10 Schedules. Promptly after award the contractor prepares and submits a construction schedule and a submittal schedule for the architect’s approval.
  • §3.12.10 Delegated design. Where the documents require the contractor to provide professional services, the architect specifies the performance and design criteria; the contractor’s design professional seals and signs the submittals; the architect is entitled to rely on their adequacy.
  • §3.18 Indemnification. The contractor indemnifies the owner, the architect and their consultants against claims for bodily injury and property damage arising out of performance of the Work, to the extent caused by the negligent acts or omissions of the contractor — but only to that extent, and not for the indemnitee’s own negligence.
Who pulls the permit? The two documents disagree — and both are right

A201 §3.7.1: the contractor secures and pays for the building permit. B101 §3.1.6: the architect assists the owner in connection with the owner’s responsibility for filing documents required for approval of governmental authorities. These are not in conflict — they describe different things. Filing the documents for design approval is the owner’s responsibility with the architect’s assistance; obtaining the building permit for construction is the contractor’s. An item asking “who is responsible for the building permit?” wants the contractor.

Article 4 — the architect’s job, written into a contract it never signs

§4.2.2 Site visitsThe architect visits at intervals appropriate to the stage of construction to become generally familiar with progress and quality and to determine in general whether the Work observed will be in accordance with the documents when completed. Not required to make exhaustive or continuous on-site inspections. The same language as B101 §3.6.2.1 — the two documents are deliberately coordinated.
§4.2.3 ReportingKeep the owner reasonably informed; report known deviations from the documents and from the most recent construction schedule, and defects and deficiencies observed.
§4.2.4 CommunicationsCommunications run through the architect. The 2017 edition permits direct owner–contractor communication — previously discouraged — but the parties must keep the architect included.
§4.2.6 Rejecting WorkThe architect has authority to reject Work that does not conform, and may require additional inspection or testing. Exercising or declining to exercise that authority creates no duty to the contractor, subcontractors, suppliers or their agents.
§4.2.7 SubmittalsReview is only for conformance with the information given and the design concept expressed in the documents — the same limited purpose as B101 §3.6.4.2.
§4.2.10 Project representativeThe owner may employ a full-time project representative at the site; duties and limitations are set out in a separate written agreement.
§4.2.11–12 InterpretationsThe architect interprets the documents on written request from either owner or contractor, responding with reasonable promptness. Interpretations must be consistent with and reasonably inferable from the documents, and the architect must endeavor to secure faithful performance by both owner and contractor and shall not show partiality to either.
§4.2.13 Aesthetic effectThe architect’s decisions on matters relating to aesthetic effect are final, if consistent with the intent expressed in the Contract Documents. One of the few places the architect’s word simply ends the question.

Seven, seven, and seven more

The whole payment cascade runs on sevens: 7 days to certify, 7 days to pay, 7 additional days’ notice before the contractor may stop work. Pair it with B101 Article 9, where every termination and suspension notice is also seven days, and with A101 §5.2.2, where final payment is due 30 days after the final Certificate for Payment.

§9.5 — the grounds for withholding certification

The architect may withhold a certificate in whole or in part, to the extent reasonably necessary to protect the owner, if in its opinion the representations required by §9.4.2 cannot be made. The listed grounds:

  1. Defective Work not remedied;
  2. Third-party claims filed, or reasonable evidence indicating probable filing;
  3. Failure to pay subcontractors or suppliers for labour, materials or equipment;
  4. Reasonable evidence that the Work cannot be completed for the unpaid balance of the contract sum;
  5. Damage to the owner or a Separate Contractor;
  6. Reasonable evidence that the Work will not be completed within the Contract Time, and that the unpaid balance would not be adequate to cover actual or liquidated damages for the anticipated delay; or
  7. Repeated failure to carry out the Work in accordance with the Contract Documents.
Withholding is surgical, not punitive

Two things the exam tests. First, the architect withholds only the amount reasonably necessary to protect the owner — not the whole application. “Withhold the entire payment” is almost always wrong where a discrete problem is identified. Second, when the reasons for withholding are removed, certification is made for amounts previously withheld (§9.5.3). Withholding is leverage toward a remedy, not a penalty.

§9.8 and §9.10 — completion

  • §9.8.2: when the contractor considers the Work substantially complete, it prepares and submits a comprehensive list of items to be completed or corrected — the punch list. Failure to include an item does not alter the obligation to complete the Work.
  • §9.8.3: the architect inspects to determine whether the Work is substantially complete. If it is not, the architect notifies the contractor of the reasons; the contractor completes or corrects and requests another inspection.
  • §9.8.4–5: when the Work is substantially complete, the architect prepares a Certificate of Substantial Completion (G704) fixing the date and the respective responsibilities of owner and contractor for security, maintenance, heat, utilities, damage to the Work and insurance, and the time within which the contractor shall finish the punch list.
  • §9.9 Partial occupancy or use: the owner may occupy a portion before substantial completion of the whole, provided the insurer consents and the authorities having jurisdiction approve. The parties agree in writing on responsibilities and on the correction period for that portion.
  • §9.10.1 Final payment: on the contractor’s notice that the Work is ready for final inspection and receipt of a final Application, the architect promptly makes the inspection and, finding the Work acceptable and the contract fully performed, promptly issues a final Certificate for Payment.
  • §9.10.2: final payment is conditioned on the contractor submitting an affidavit that payrolls and bills are paid, a certificate of insurance for products and completed operations, consent of surety to final payment, and data establishing payment of obligations — releases and waivers of liens.
  • §9.10.4–5: acceptance of final payment by the contractor waives all claims except those previously made in writing and identified as unsettled. Making final payment waives the owner’s claims except those arising from liens, from failure of the Work to comply with the Contract Documents, or from the terms of special warranties.

The one-year correction period is not a warranty and not a statute

§12.2.2.1: if, within one year after the date of Substantial Completion, any of the Work is found not to be in accordance with the Contract Documents, the contractor shall correct it promptly after receipt of written notice from the owner. Three corollaries the exam likes: (1) it is an obligation to correct, not a warranty — §3.5 warranties may run longer or shorter; (2) §12.2.5: the one-year period does not establish a limitation period on the contractor’s other obligations — the statute of limitations is a separate question; (3) the owner must give notice promptly after discovery, and failing to do so can forfeit the right to require correction.

§12.3 Acceptance of nonconforming Work: the owner may choose to accept work that is not in accordance rather than require its removal, in which case the contract sum is reduced — whether or not final payment has been made. Note the parallel to betterment in PcM: the remedy tracks the value of the difference, not the cost of perfection.

What actually changed between A201–2007 and A201–2017

Insurance: the detailed requirements moved out of Article 11 into an exhibit to the owner–contractor agreement. Notice (§1.6): consolidated, and all notice must be in writing — claims notice by certified or registered mail or courier. Concealed conditions: notice shortened from 21 days to 14. Digital data: E203 is incorporated, and a party using digital data without agreed protocols cannot rely on it. Owner–contractor direct communication is now permitted. Termination for convenience: a negotiated termination fee replaces the old overhead-and-profit formula. Claims: those arising before the end of the correction period need 21-day notice and an initial decision; those arising after need notice but no deadline and no initial decision. Separate Contractor becomes a defined term, and the IDM is insulated from liability for good-faith interpretations.

Two places A201 and B101 must be read side by side

Site visits: A201 §4.2.2 and B101 §3.6.2.1 use the same words — visit, generally familiar, in general, not exhaustive or continuous. That is deliberate: the architect owes the owner a duty under B101 and is described to the contractor in identical terms under A201. Certificates for payment: B101 §3.6.3 says what certifying means to the owner; A201 §9.4 and §9.5 say when it must happen and when it may be withheld. Learn them as one subject, not two.

PjM 3.2C401 — Architect–Consultant, and the flow-down

  • C401 incorporates the prevailing owner–architect agreement by reference, so the consultant owes the architect obligations parallel to those the architect owes the owner — standard of care, scope, schedule, termination, dispute resolution, insurance. That is the flow-down provision.
  • Its purpose is symmetry: the architect can only recover from a consultant what it can be held to by the owner. A raised standard of care in the owner–architect agreement must flow down, or the architect absorbs the gap.
  • Each consultant is judged against the standard of care of its own discipline — a structural engineer against structural engineers.
  • The architect is nevertheless responsible to the owner for coordinating all consultants into one set of documents, and can face contractual exposure for a consultant’s error because the architect contracted for the whole scope of design services. Indemnity from the consultant does not help if the consultant is insolvent or under-insured.
  • For owner-hired consultants the exposure is far narrower: the architect is generally not responsible — unless it agrees to be in the owner–architect agreement, or acts in a way that assumes responsibility, such as signing a Certificate for Payment or a Certificate of Substantial Completion covering that consultant’s work.
Two related forms people confuse

C101 Joint Venture — two or more firms jointly hold the prime design contract; courts commonly treat JV parties as jointly and severally liable. C102 Teaming Agreement — collaboration during the pursuit, before any prime contract exists. Neither is C401.

PjM 2.3Reading a real agreement — how to spot a modified one

Every AIA form you meet in practice has been marked up by somebody. You do not need to master this for the exam — but you need it the first week of a job, and one or two PcM items turn on recognising an uninsurable clause. Learn the standard first; treat everything else as a deviation from it.

  • AIA software prints an Additions and Deletions Report at the back of a modified document, and puts a vertical line in the left margin beside every altered paragraph. Read that report before the agreement.
  • “Intentionally deleted” or “(Paragraphs deleted)” marks a clause someone removed. Ask what protection went with it — a missing consequential-damages waiver or a missing mediation clause is a deletion that costs the architect real money.
  • Watch for a raised standard of care: “highest,” “best,” “most stringent,” “warrant,” “guarantee,” “ensure.” Generally uninsurable.
  • Watch for ownership of documents replacing copyrights and licenses — that hands the architect’s copyright to the owner.
  • Watch for broad indemnities that cover the owner’s own negligence, and for certificates that operate as warranties. Both are outside a typical E&O policy.
  • Watch for hard day counts inserted where the standard says “reasonable promptness” — a fixed 7-day turnaround on every submittal and RFI is a performance obligation the architect may not be staffed to meet.
  • Owner-added articles — performance evaluation and scoring, affirmative-action plans, records access, statutory claims procedures, fixed venue — are public-owner boilerplate, not AIA text.
The exam-day rule

If a question asks what the owner–architect agreement says, answer with the AIA standard. If a question describes an owner proposing one of the edits above, the correct response is to negotiate it out, or flag it to your insurance broker and counsel before signing — because most of those edits are uninsurable, and an uninsured obligation is one you pay personally.
