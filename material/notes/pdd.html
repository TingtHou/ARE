Section 1 · Integration of Building Materials & Systems 31–37%

PDD 1.5The control layers — the organising idea for every envelope detail

Every enclosure does five things: support, rain control, air control, thermal control, vapour control. Detail each one as a continuous plane and trace it round the whole building without a break — that tracing exercise is what enclosure items actually test.

Rainscreen, outside to insideCladding (mechanically sheds most rain) → ventilated air space → drainage plane, draining to flashing → exterior insulation → water/air/vapour control layer → the proper enclosure (OSB, CMU, exterior gypsum) → structure → interior finish. The shield takes the hit; the drainage plane catches what gets past; the control layer is the last line and must be continuous.

The vapour rule that matters: avoid two vapour-impermeable layers in one assembly, because water trapped between them cannot dry in either direction. Note that sealed rain-control layers — membranes and fluid-applied barriers — are vapour impermeable, so in a masonry rainscreen or a low-slope roof the rain barrier is the vapour control layer. Do not then add a second one.

Thermal bridging: metal, glass and plastic elements outboard of the insulation are cold in winter and condense when warm interior air reaches them. The mirror case in hot-humid climates: vinyl wallpaper and marker boards inboard of the insulation are cold in summer and condense on their back face.

Correction — "Grade 60 rebar has 60,000 psi tensile strength"

Your Amber Book workbook states this. Grade 60 designates a minimum YIELD strength of 60,000 psi, not tensile strength. The minimum tensile strength of Grade 60 bar is substantially higher — around 90,000 psi under ASTM A615. Yield is where steel stops behaving elastically; tensile is where it breaks. Design is based on yield. This is a structural-terminology error that would propagate straight into a wrong answer.

Correction — "control joints spaced every 5 feet or so"

Your Panic Notes say this of concrete slabs generally. It is too tight and not how spacing is determined. Olin's own rule of thumb is joint spacing in feet no more than twice the slab thickness in inches — a 4 in slab gets joints at 8 ft, a 6 in slab at 12 ft. Sidewalks are the case closest to 5 ft, at roughly the slab width and 6 ft maximum. Use the thickness rule.

Joints, and what each one doesControl / contraction joint — partial depth, induces the crack where you want it. Isolation / expansion joint — full depth, separates two elements entirely so they move independently (slab to wall, slab to column, breaking a long building into segments). Construction joint — where one pour stops and the next begins; the two are meant to act together. Partial depth = crack control. Full depth = separation.

Portland cement types, ASTM C150: I normal, IA normal air-entrained, II moderate sulfate resistance, IIA same air-entrained, III high early strength, IIIA same air-entrained, IV low heat of hydration, V high sulfate resistance.

Correction — these are cement types, not concrete types, and it is sulfate not sulfite

Your Panic Notes head this list "Types of Concrete" and spell the chemistry "Sulfites." Both are wrong. Types I–V are portland cement types under ASTM C150 — one ingredient of concrete, alongside aggregate, water and admixtures. And the chemistry is sulfate (SO₄), not sulfite. Type II has moderate sulfate resistance, Type V high sulfate resistance — reach for them where groundwater or soil is sulfate-bearing.

Concrete sets within about 24 hours but does not reach specified strength for 28 days. Wetter mixes are more workable and weaker; drier mixes are stronger and harder to place. Normal-weight structural concrete is commonly 3,000–4,000 psi; lightweight aggregate (expanded shale, vermiculite, pumice) reduces weight 25–80% at the cost of strength. Bottom bars resist bending at midspan; stirrups resist diagonal shear near the supports.

PDD 1.5Wood, steel and roofing

Nominal vs actual lumber: nominal 1 and 5/4 are ¼ in less than nominal; nominal 2, 3, 4, 5 and 6 are ½ in less; nominal 8 and above are ¾ in less. Board feet = (thickness × width × length in feet) ÷ 12, in nominal dimensions — a 1×10 eight feet long is 1 × 10 × 8 ÷ 12 = 6.67 board feet.

Framing limits Consistent across your sources and with the IRC: stud notches no more than 25% of stud depth in a bearing wall; bored holes no more than 40%; joist notches no more than ⅙ of joist depth, and never in the middle third of the span. Platform (Western) framing interrupts studs at each floor; balloon framing runs studs continuously past the floor.

Engineered wood, weakest and cheapest to strongest: LSL and OSL (shredded strands) → PSL (parallel strands — stronger because of strand geometry) → glulam (laminated strips) → CLT (alternating layers, making a two-way panel). Plywood uses an odd number of plies so the face grains balance and the panel does not warp. The more processed the wood product, the more glue, the higher the embodied energy.

Preservative treatment: creosote — oil-based, cannot be painted, no longer used in new construction; pentachlorophenol — oil-based, telephone poles; CCA — phased out for toxicity; ACQ/PTI — current, and more corrosive to fasteners, so use hot-dip galvanised or stainless; borates (SBX, DOT) — resist termites but not rot, and leach if wetted.

Correction — the attic ventilation rule in your Panic Notes merges two separate provisions

They state: "minimum vent area equal to 1/150 of attic footprint with ½ the vent area placed at least 3 feet above the eave." Those are two different rules and the second one belongs to an exception. The base requirement is 1/150. The reduction to 1/300 is permitted where a Class I or II vapour retarder is installed on the warm side, or where at least 40% and not more than 50% of the required venting is in the upper portion, at least 3 feet above the eave. So "½ the vent area at least 3 ft above the eave" is roughly the condition that earns you 1/300 — not something you do on top of 1/150. And it is 40–50%, not exactly half.

Steel: a connection at the web only is generally a shear connection; adding connections at the flanges makes it a moment connection. Welding costs more than bolting, and field welding costs more than shop welding. A flag on a welding symbol means the weld is made in the field. Bolting: bearing-type bolts are tightened to snug fit; slip-critical connections and direct tension indicators are used where bolt tension must be assured.

Galvanic corrosion — the order that mattersThe series, anodic (corrodes) to cathodic (protected): aluminum → zinc → steel → iron → stainless (active) → tin → lead → copper → stainless (passive). The farther apart two metals sit, the worse the couple. Aluminum touching copper is the classic failure — an aluminum nail through copper flashing destroys the nail. Separate dissimilar metals with a gasket, coating or non-conductive washer. Aluminum does not rust but it does oxidise; anodising is a controlled oxidation.

F rating vs T ratingF is about flame; T is about temperature. An F rating says fire did not pass through. A T rating adds that the unexposed side — including the pipe or conduit itself — stayed within 325 °F of ambient, so combustibles touching it will not ignite. A penetration through a floor generally needs both; one through a wall often needs only F. If an item mentions storage against the wall on the other side, it is asking for a T rating.

Fire vs smoke dampers: a fire damper closes on heat, in a duct penetrating a fire-rated assembly. A smoke damper closes on a signal from a smoke detector, in a smoke barrier. Correction — smoke alarm types

Your Panic Notes recommend "combination ionization/photovoltaic smoke alarms." The word is photoelectric, not photovoltaic. Alternate vs substitutionAn alternate is named in the specification and priced as part of the bid — the owner asked to see the cost of a second option. Language. Use the imperative mood — the verb leads: "Spread adhesive with a notched trowel." Concise and unambiguous. Correction — minimum ceiling height, and which code you are in

Your Panic Notes say "Minimum ceiling height is 7′ tall, but you can go down to 6′8″ for no more than half of the ceiling area." That merges the IRC and the IBC. Under the IBC the general occupiable-space minimum is 7 ft 6 in, with 7 ft for bathrooms, toilet rooms, kitchens, storage rooms and laundry rooms. The 7 ft figure alone is the IRC's habitable-space minimum. The "half the area" rule belongs to sloped ceilings, and the 6 ft 8 in figure belongs to projections such as beams and ducts. Note: in the 2021 IBC this moved to §1208.2 — it is no longer §1207.2, which several older guides cite.

Correction — fire lane dimensions

Your Panic Notes give fire lanes as 20 ft wide, 10 ft from a building overhang, 54 ft outside and 30 ft inside turning radius, carrying 35 tons. The 20 ft minimum unobstructed width is standard (with 13 ft 6 in vertical clearance). The turning radii are jurisdiction-specific — the International Fire Code leaves them to the fire code official, so no universal number exists. The load figure commonly cited is 75,000 lb of imposed fire apparatus load, not 70,000 lb (35 tons). Do not memorise the radii as though they were code.

Correction — "Type I is the most restrictive to build with"

Your Panic Notes say this. It reads backwards. Type I is the most fire-resistant and therefore the LEAST restricted in allowable height and area — it buys you the tallest, biggest building. What is restricted is the palette of materials you may use, because Type I requires noncombustible construction. Say it as: Type I restricts your materials and rewards you with height and area; Type V frees your materials and penalises you on both.

Section 5 · Construction Cost Estimates 2–8%

Two to eight items. The whole objective is: read an estimate at CD stage and say whether it matches the documents.

  • At CD stage you expect an AACE Class 2 or Class 1 estimate — detailed unit price, MasterFormat-organised, built from a full quantity takeoff, with accuracy in the range of −5 to +15% (Class 2) or −3 to +10% (Class 1).
  • An estimate that is still organised by UniFormat at CD stage is a design-phase estimate that has not been re-based — that is a finding.
  • Check that the estimate carries general conditions, contractor overhead and profit, escalation to the midpoint of construction, and a contingency appropriate to the phase — a contingency that has not come down since DD is a finding.
  • Check that alternates and allowances in the estimate match the ones in the specifications, and that unit prices exist for every uncertain-quantity item.
  • Check that scope in the drawings appears in the estimate at all. The most common real finding is an omission, not a bad unit price.

Life-cycle cost (ASTM E917): LCC = I + Repl − Res + E + W + OM&R + O — initial investment, capital replacement, less residual value, energy, water, non-fuel operations/maintenance/repair, and other, all in present value. Simple payback ignores the time value of money; discounted payback includes it. Savings-to-investment ratio above 1.0 indicates a cost-effective investment. IRR is the discount rate at which net present value equals zero. NIST's own caution is quotable: a shorter payback period does not by itself indicate the better long-term investment.
